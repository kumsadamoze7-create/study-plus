UNIT 3: STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM

3.1 Historical Development of Atomic Theories of Matter

The Indivisible Atom

The word atom comes from the Greek word atomos, meaning indivisible or cannot be divided.

Ancient philosophers discussed whether matter could be divided forever or whether there was a smallest possible particle.

The Greek Concept of Atomos

Leucippus

Leucippus was an ancient Greek philosopher who proposed that matter consists of very small particles and empty space. He is often considered one of the earliest thinkers associated with atomism.

Democritus

Democritus developed the idea of atomism further. He proposed that matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atomos.

Main ideas of Democritus:

  • Matter is made of atoms.
  • Atoms are extremely small.
  • Atoms are indivisible.
  • Atoms are permanent.
  • Atoms differ in size and shape.
  • Atoms move through empty space.

Five Major Points of the Early Atomic Idea

  1. Matter consists of very small particles.
  2. Atoms cannot be divided.
  3. Atoms are permanent.
  4. Atoms differ in size and shape.
  5. Atoms move through empty space.

Drawbacks of Early Greek Philosophers

  • Their ideas were mainly philosophical.
  • They did not have experimental evidence.
  • They could not describe the internal structure of atoms.
  • They could not explain chemical reactions completely.
  • The statement that atoms are indivisible was later proved wrong.

3.2 Three Fundamental Laws of Chemical Reactions

Three important laws helped scientists understand chemical reactions and later contributed to the development of Dalton's atomic theory.

1. Law of Conservation of Mass

Antoine Lavoisier

Antoine Lavoisier was a French chemist who established the Law of Conservation of Mass through careful quantitative experiments.

Law of Conservation of Mass: Mass is neither created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction. The total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
Mass of reactants = Mass of products
Example:
If 10 g of substance A reacts with 5 g of substance B, the total mass of products is 15 g, assuming no material is lost.

2. Law of Definite Proportions

Joseph Louis Proust

Joseph Louis Proust was a French chemist who formulated the Law of Definite Proportions.

Law of Definite Proportions: A pure compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass.
Example:
Pure water always contains hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed mass ratio, regardless of where the water comes from.

3. Law of Multiple Proportions

John Dalton

John Dalton was an English chemist and physicist who proposed the Law of Multiple Proportions and later developed the first modern scientific atomic theory.

Law of Multiple Proportions: When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.
Example:
Carbon and oxygen form CO and CO₂. For a fixed amount of carbon, the amount of oxygen in CO₂ is twice that in CO.

3.3 Atomic Theory

Dalton's Atomic Theory

John Dalton (1766–1844)

John Dalton was an English scientist who developed the first scientific atomic theory in the early 1800s.

His theory was based on experimental observations and the three fundamental laws of chemical reactions.

Dalton's Postulates

  1. All matter is composed of extremely small particles called atoms.
  2. Atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties.
  3. Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.
  4. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
  5. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed during ordinary chemical reactions; they are rearranged.

Drawbacks of Dalton's Atomic Theory

  • Atoms are not indivisible because they contain subatomic particles.
  • Atoms of the same element may have different masses. These are called isotopes.
  • Atoms of different elements can sometimes have the same mass number.
  • Atoms can be changed in nuclear reactions.

Modern Atomic Theory

Modern atomic theory developed after the discovery of electrons, protons, neutrons, the nucleus and other evidence about atomic structure.

Modern Atomic Theory Postulates

  • Matter is composed of atoms.
  • Atoms contain subatomic particles.
  • Atoms of the same element have the same atomic number.
  • Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons.
  • Atoms can gain or lose electrons to form ions.
  • Atoms combine to form molecules and compounds.
  • Atoms can undergo nuclear changes.

Dalton vs Modern Atomic Theory

Dalton Modern Theory
Atom is indivisible. Atom contains subatomic particles.
Atoms of the same element are identical in mass. Isotopes of an element can have different masses.
Atoms cannot be changed. Nuclear reactions can change atoms.
No knowledge of nucleus. Atom contains a nucleus.

3.4 Discovery of Fundamental Subatomic Particles and Atomic Nucleus

The atom was once considered indivisible. Experiments later showed that atoms contain smaller particles. These are called subatomic particles.

The Three Fundamental Subatomic Particles

Particle Charge Relative Mass Location
Electron -1 Very small Outside nucleus
Proton +1 Approximately 1 amu Nucleus
Neutron 0 Approximately 1 amu Nucleus

Discovery of the Electron

J. J. Thomson (1856–1940)

J. J. Thomson was a British physicist who discovered the electron in 1897 through experiments using cathode ray tubes.

Cathode Ray Experiment

Thomson used a cathode ray tube containing gas at low pressure. When a high voltage was applied, rays travelled from the cathode toward the anode.

The rays were deflected by electric and magnetic fields. From this Thomson concluded that cathode rays were made of negatively charged particles.

Conclusion: The electron is a negatively charged subatomic particle.

Discovery of the Proton

Ernest Rutherford

Ernest Rutherford was a New Zealand-born physicist who identified the positively charged particle associated with the hydrogen nucleus and contributed greatly to the discovery of the atomic nucleus.

Rutherford's work with positive rays and nuclear experiments provided important evidence for the existence of positively charged particles.

Proton: A positively charged subatomic particle found in the nucleus.

Discovery of the Neutron

James Chadwick (1891–1974)

James Chadwick was a British physicist who discovered the neutron in 1932.

He bombarded beryllium with alpha particles and observed a penetrating radiation. Experiments showed that this radiation consisted of neutral particles with a mass close to that of a proton.

Neutron: A subatomic particle with no electric charge found in the nucleus.

Discovery of the Atomic Nucleus

Ernest Rutherford

Rutherford discovered the existence of the small, dense atomic nucleus through the famous gold foil experiment.

Gold Foil Experiment

Rutherford and his colleagues directed alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil. Some were deflected, and a very small number bounced back.

Conclusions

  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • The positive charge is concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
  • Most of the atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus.
  • The nucleus is extremely small compared with the whole atom.

Bohr Atomic Model

Niels Bohr (1885–1962)

Niels Bohr was a Danish physicist who proposed a model of the atom in 1913.

He suggested that electrons move around the nucleus in specific energy levels or shells.

Main Ideas of Bohr's Model

  • Electrons occupy specific energy levels.
  • Electrons do not normally exist between allowed energy levels.
  • An electron can absorb energy and move to a higher level.
  • An electron can release energy and move to a lower level.

Millikan Oil Drop Experiment

Robert Millikan (1868–1953)

Robert Millikan was an American physicist who performed the oil-drop experiment.

The experiment measured the charge of the electron and helped determine its fundamental electric charge.

Importance

  • Determined the charge of an electron.
  • Provided strong evidence that electric charge is quantized.
  • Combined with Thomson's charge-to-mass measurement, it helped determine the electron's mass.

3.4 Composition of the Atom and Isotopes

Electron

  • Symbol: e⁻
  • Charge: -1
  • Located outside the nucleus.
  • Very small mass.

Proton

  • Symbol: p⁺
  • Charge: +1
  • Located in the nucleus.
  • Mass ≈ 1 amu.

Neutron

  • Symbol: n⁰
  • Charge: 0
  • Located in the nucleus.
  • Mass ≈ 1 amu.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
Example: Carbon-12 and Carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon. Both have 6 protons, but they have different numbers of neutrons.
Isotope Protons Neutrons Electrons (neutral atom)
Carbon-12 6 6 6
Carbon-14 6 8 6

3.5 Atomic Number and Atomic Mass

Atomic Number

Atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
Z = Number of protons

For a neutral atom:

Number of protons = Number of electrons

Mass Number

Mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
A = Protons + Neutrons
Therefore:
Neutrons = A - Z

Atomic Mass and Isotopes

An element can have more than one isotope. Therefore, the atomic mass shown on the periodic table is usually a weighted average of the masses of its naturally occurring isotopes.

Average Atomic Mass

Average atomic mass is the weighted average of the masses of the naturally occurring isotopes of an element.
Average atomic mass = (mass₁ × abundance₁) + (mass₂ × abundance₂) + ...

When percentage abundance is used, divide each percentage by 100.

Suppose an element has two isotopes: Isotope A = 10 amu, abundance = 20% Isotope B = 11 amu, abundance = 80%

Average atomic mass = (10 × 0.20) + (11 × 0.80)
= 2 + 8.8
= 10.8 amu

Electron Configuration on Main Shells

Electrons are arranged around the nucleus in main energy levels or shells.

Main Energy Levels

Shell Principal Level Maximum Electrons
K 1 2
L 2 8
M 3 18
N 4 32

The maximum number of electrons in a shell can be calculated using:

Maximum electrons = 2n²

where n is the principal energy level.

Examples

Hydrogen (Z = 1)
Electron configuration: 1
Helium (Z = 2)
Electron configuration: 2
Carbon (Z = 6)
Electron configuration: 2, 4
Oxygen (Z = 8)
Electron configuration: 2, 6
Sodium (Z = 11)
Electron configuration: 2, 8, 1
Chlorine (Z = 17)
Electron configuration: 2, 8, 7

Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost occupied main energy level of an atom.
Element Atomic Number Configuration Valence Electrons
Li 3 2,1 1
C 6 2,4 4
O 8 2,6 6
Ne 10 2,8 8
Na 11 2,8,1 1
Cl 17 2,8,7 7
