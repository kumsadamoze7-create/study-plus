Unit 9 — Statistics and Probability

📚 Complete Study Notes

📌 Introduction

Statistics is the branch of mathematics concerned with collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data.

Probability is the branch of mathematics used to measure the chance or likelihood that an event will happen.

9.1 Statistical Data

What is Data?

Data are facts, observations or measurements collected for a particular purpose.

Example:

The marks of five students are: 70, 65, 80, 75, 90.

These numbers are statistical data.

Collection of Statistical Data

Data collection is the process of gathering information that will be used for analysis and decision making.

Types of Data

  • Qualitative data: Describes qualities or categories.
  • Quantitative data: Expressed using numbers.
  • Discrete data: Countable values such as number of students.
  • Continuous data: Measurements such as height, weight and temperature.

📥 Sources of Data

Primary Data

Primary data are collected directly by the researcher for a specific purpose.

Examples include:

  • Interview
  • Questionnaire
  • Direct observation
  • Experiment

Secondary Data

Secondary data are data that have already been collected by another person or organization.

Examples:

  • Books
  • Government reports
  • Newspapers
  • Research reports
  • Websites and databases

Interview

An interview is a method of collecting information by asking questions directly to people.

Direct Observation

Direct observation means collecting information by watching and recording events or behaviors directly.

📋 Organizing Data

After collecting data, it should be organized so that it can be understood easily.

Common methods include:

  • Arranging data in ascending or descending order
  • Tables
  • Frequency distributions
  • Graphs and charts

Frequency

Frequency is the number of times a particular value or category occurs.

Data: 2, 3, 2, 4, 3, 2

Frequency of 2 = 3

Frequency of 3 = 2

Frequency of 4 = 1

📈 Presentation of Data

Statistical data can be presented using:

  • Tables
  • Bar charts
  • Pie charts
  • Histograms
  • Frequency distributions

Analysis of Data

Analysis means examining collected data to identify patterns, relationships and important information.

Interpretation of Data

Interpretation means explaining what the analyzed data tell us.

9.1.2 Graphical Presentation of Statistical Data

🥧 Pie Chart

A pie chart is a circular graph divided into sectors. Each sector represents a category of the data.

Central angle = (Frequency ÷ Total frequency) × 360°
Suppose 20 students are surveyed and 5 like football. Central angle: (5 ÷ 20) × 360° = 90°

📊 Frequency Distribution

A frequency distribution is a table that shows the values or classes of data together with their frequencies.

Score Frequency
1 3
2 5
3 4
4 2

📊 Histogram

A histogram is a graph used to represent frequency distribution for continuous or grouped data.

  • Bars touch each other.
  • The horizontal axis represents class intervals.
  • The vertical axis represents frequency.
Remember:

In a histogram, the bars normally have no gaps because the class intervals are continuous.

9.1.3 Measures of Central Tendency

Measures of central tendency describe the center or typical value of a data set.

The three main measures are:

  • Mean
  • Median
  • Mode

Mean

The mean is obtained by adding all observations and dividing by the number of observations.

Mean = Sum of observations ÷ Number of observations
Data: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 Sum = 40 Number of observations = 5 Mean = 40 ÷ 5 = 8

Median

The median is the middle value after the data have been arranged in order.

If the number of observations is odd, there is one middle value.

If the number is even, the median is the average of the two middle values.

Data: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 Median = 6
Data: 2, 4, 6, 8 Median = (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5

Mode

The mode is the value that occurs most frequently.

Data: 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 3, 6 Mode = 3

Properties

  • A data set may have one mode.
  • A data set may have more than one mode.
  • A data set may have no mode.
  • The mean uses every observation.
  • The median is less affected by extreme values than the mean.

9.1.4 Measures of Dispersion

Dispersion describes how spread out the data values are.

Range

Range = Maximum value − Minimum value
Data: 4, 7, 9, 12, 15 Range = 15 − 4 Range = 11

Variance

Variance measures the average squared distance of the observations from the mean.

Population Variance: σ² = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N

Standard Deviation

Standard deviation is the positive square root of variance.

σ = √[Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N]

🧮 Steps for Calculating Standard Deviation

  1. Find the mean.
  2. Subtract the mean from each observation.
  3. Square each deviation.
  4. Add all squared deviations.
  5. Divide by the number of observations.
  6. Take the square root.
Example: Data: 2, 4, 6 Mean: (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = 4 Deviations: 2−4 = −2 4−4 = 0 6−4 = 2 Squares: 4, 0, 4 Sum = 8 Variance: 8 ÷ 3 = 2.67 Standard deviation: √2.67 ≈ 1.63

Properties of Standard Deviation

  • It is always non-negative.
  • A smaller standard deviation means data are closer to the mean.
  • A larger standard deviation means data are more spread out.
  • Standard deviation is zero when all values are equal.

9.2 Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is to occur.

Probability is between 0 and 1.

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

Probability 0 means impossible and probability 1 means certain.

🎲 Experimental Probability

Experimental probability is calculated from the results of an actual experiment.

Experimental Probability = Number of times event occurs ÷ Total number of trials
A coin is tossed 20 times and heads occurs 12 times. P(Heads) = 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6

🔵 Sample Space and Event

Sample Space

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

For tossing a coin: S = {H, T}
For rolling a die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Event

An event is a set of one or more outcomes from the sample space.

For a die, getting an even number: A = {2, 4, 6}

🎯 Probability of an Event

For equally likely outcomes:

P(A) = Number of favorable outcomes ÷ Total number of possible outcomes
A fair die is rolled. Probability of getting 4: P(4) = 1 ÷ 6
Probability of getting an even number: Favorable outcomes = {2,4,6} P(Even) = 3 ÷ 6 = 1/2

🔄 Complement of an Event

The complement of an event A means that A does not occur.

P(A') = 1 − P(A)
If P(A) = 0.3, P(A') = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7

🌳 Tree Diagram

A tree diagram is a diagram used to display all possible outcomes of a sequence of events.

Example

Suppose a coin is tossed twice.

  • First toss: H or T
  • Second toss: H or T

Possible outcomes:

HH, HT, TH, TT

There are 4 possible outcomes.

📐 Theoretical Probability

Theoretical probability is based on mathematical reasoning rather than performing an experiment.

P(A) = Favorable outcomes ÷ Total equally likely outcomes
A standard die has six equally likely outcomes. Probability of rolling a number greater than 4: Favorable outcomes = {5,6} P(A) = 2 ÷ 6 P(A) = 1/3

📝 Unit 9 Quick Summary

  • Data: collected information.
  • Primary data: collected directly.
  • Secondary data: obtained from existing sources.
  • Mean: total ÷ number of observations.
  • Median: middle value.
  • Mode: most frequent value.
  • Range: maximum − minimum.
  • Variance: measure of squared spread.
  • Standard deviation: square root of variance.
  • Sample space: all possible outcomes.
  • Event: one or more outcomes.
  • Experimental probability: based on experiment results.
  • Theoretical probability: based on equally likely outcomes.
  • Tree diagram: shows possible outcomes of successive events.
