UNIT 5

🌍 PEOPLES AND STATES OF AFRICA TO 1500

📚 Detailed Topic-by-Topic Notes

📖 Unit 5 Introduction

Africa is a vast continent with many peoples, languages, cultures, societies and states. Before colonial rule, many African civilizations and political states developed in different parts of the continent.

This unit examines the peoples and language families of Africa, states in North Africa, the spread and impact of Islam, West African empires, states in Central, Eastern and Southern Africa, African relations and trade.

🎯 Unit Learning Outcomes

  • Describe major political, economic, social and cultural developments in pre-colonial Africa.
  • Describe the peoples and language families of Africa.
  • Explain relationships created by trade, migration and warfare.
  • Explain the rise and decline of African empires.
  • Explain the origin and impact of Islam in Africa.
  • Understand African relations with other continents.

🌍 5.1 Languages and Peoples of Africa

Africa is inhabited by peoples who speak many different languages. The textbook notes that more than 1,000 diverse and interrelated languages are spoken across the continent.

Four major language super-families:
  1. Afro-Asiatic
  2. Niger-Congo / Congo-Kordofanian
  3. Nilo-Saharan
  4. Khoisan

1️⃣ Afro-Asiatic

The Afro-Asiatic language family is mainly found in North Africa, the Sahara, the Horn of Africa and around Lake Chad.

Main sub-groups:

  • Berber
  • Chadic
  • Coptic / Ancient Egyptian
  • Cushitic
  • Omotic
  • Semitic

Semitic languages are mainly spoken in the Horn of Africa. Omotic languages are spoken only in Ethiopia. Cushitic languages extend from the Red Sea region through the Horn of Africa toward Kenya and Tanzania.

2️⃣ Niger-Congo / Congo-Kordofanian

Niger-Congo covers much of West Africa and much of sub-Saharan Africa.

Bantu

Bantu languages form a major branch of Niger-Congo. Bantu-speaking peoples live across large parts of sub-Saharan Africa.

Kiswahili belongs to the Bantu language group and has strong Arabic influence as a result of historical contact and trade.

3️⃣ Nilo-Saharan

Nilo-Saharan is another major African language super-family.

Major sub-groups include:

  • Chari-Nile
  • Songhay
  • Saharan
  • Meban
  • Koman
  • Fur

4️⃣ Khoisan

The Khoisan language family includes two major groups: Khoikhoi and San.

Their speakers historically lived around the Kalahari region, including parts of southern Africa.

🌐 Non-native languages

Not every language spoken in Africa originated in Africa. Arabic spread into North Africa with Muslim Arab expansion. Later European colonialism introduced languages such as English, French, Spanish and Portuguese into different parts of Africa.

🏛️ 5.2 States in North Africa

🇪🇬 A. Mamluk Egypt

The Mamluks were soldiers of mainly Turkish origin who gradually became powerful military commanders in Egypt.

1250–1517: Mamluk dynasty ruled Egypt.

The Mamluks expanded their influence into areas including Palestine, Syria and other regions.

Economic development

Egypt was an important center of agriculture, trade and commercial activity.

Ethiopia–Egypt relations

Relations between Mamluk Egypt and Christian Ethiopia were often characterized by conflict, although Ethiopian ruler Zera Yacob attempted to establish smoother relations.

Reasons for Mamluk decline

  • Corruption
  • Decline in agricultural productivity
  • Cattle plague
  • Drought
  • Military weakness against the Ottomans
1517: The Ottoman Turks defeated the Mamluks and occupied Egypt.

🇸🇩 B. Funj Sultanate

The Funj Sultanate was established in northeast Africa, in the area of present-day Sudan.

1504: Funj Sultanate established its capital at Sennar.

The Funj were originally cattle-keeping nomads. Their state became involved in trade and political rivalry with neighboring states.

Relations with Ethiopia

The Funj fought against the Christian kingdom of Ethiopia, particularly because of competition over trade routes.

The textbook notes that King Susenyos's army defeated the Funj around 1620, although the Funj Sultanate survived until the 19th century.

☪️ 5.3 Spread of Islam and Its Impact in West Africa

📌 Origin and expansion

Islam originated in the Arabian Peninsula in the seventh century. Muslim Arab armies conquered Egypt in 642 AD and later expanded across North Africa.

Islam eventually reached West Africa mainly through trade, merchants, scholars and political connections.

🕌 Impact of Islam

  • Expanded Trans-Saharan trade.
  • Connected West Africa with North Africa and the Mediterranean.
  • Muslim merchants settled in commercial centers.
  • Encouraged cultural exchange.
  • Helped spread literacy and Islamic learning.
  • Influenced political organization.
  • Contributed to the rise of powerful West African empires.
Why did rulers accept Islam?

Islam was attractive to many rulers because religious and political authority could be connected, while conversion also strengthened trade and diplomatic relationships.

👑 5.4 States in Western Africa

Several powerful states and empires developed in West Africa. The major ones discussed in the textbook are:

  • Ghana
  • Mali
  • Songhay
  • Kanem-Bornu
  • Hausa States

🇬🇭 A. Ghana Empire

Ancient Ghana was located in parts of present-day Mauritania, Mali and Senegal.

It was founded by the Soninke people and became powerful through Trans-Saharan trade.

About 1000 AD: Ghana reached the height of its power.

Economic foundation

  • Trans-Saharan trade
  • Gold
  • Salt
  • Taxes on trade

Decline

The rise of Islamic powers affected Ghana. The Almoravids invaded Ghana and captured Kumbi Saleh.

1076: Almoravids captured Kumbi Saleh.

🇲🇱 B. Mali Empire

Mali emerged after the decline of Ghana. It was founded by Sundiata Keita of the Mandinka people.

Achievements

  • Expansion of trade
  • Spread of language and customs
  • Growth of Islamic learning
  • Expansion along the Niger River

👑 Mansa Musa

Mansa Musa was the most famous ruler of Mali. He ruled from 1312–1337.

He made a famous pilgrimage to Mecca and helped expand Islamic influence in Mali.

📚 Timbuktu

Timbuktu became one of the major centers of Islamic learning and culture in West Africa.

Decline of Mali

  • Weak leadership after Mansa Musa
  • Attacks from neighboring peoples
  • Tuareg attacks

🇳🇪 C. Songhay Empire

The Songhay established their empire with its capital at Gao.

Songhay had earlier been under the influence of Ghana and Mali, but later became independent and powerful.

Sources of power

  • Control of Trans-Saharan trade
  • Gold
  • Trade with Berbers
  • Strong political organization

Songhay rulers accepted Islam early in the 11th century.

1591: Moroccan forces defeated Songhay at the Battle of Tondibi.

🐪 D. Kanem-Bornu

Kanem-Bornu developed in the grassland region of Africa. Its ruling dynasty was Muslim.

Importance of trade

  • Trade supported the state's wealth.
  • The army protected trade routes.
  • Taxes were collected from traders.

The state later weakened partly because important trade centers shifted from inland routes toward the Atlantic coast.

🏙️ E. Hausa States

Hausa city-states emerged in present-day Nigeria between about 1000 and 1200 AD.

Major Hausa city-states:

  • Kano
  • Katsina
  • Zazzau / Zaria
  • Gobir
  • Kebbi
  • Rano
  • Zamfara

Economic activities

  • Agriculture
  • Pastoralism
  • Trans-Saharan trade
  • Leather production
  • Textile production
  • Craft technology

Kano was one of the most famous Hausa cities and was surrounded by walls.

Islam spread into Hausa lands from Kanem-Bornu.

🌍 5.5 Equatorial, Central and Eastern Africa

Migration of Bantu-speaking peoples

Beginning in the first millennium BC, Bantu-speaking farming communities migrated from western Africa toward East Africa and the Congo Basin.

These migrations generally occurred gradually through small communities rather than as one massive invasion.

🇨🇩 A. Kingdom of Kongo

The Kingdom of Kongo was located along the lower Zaire (Congo) River.

Bantu peoples established the kingdom in the late fourteenth century.

Economic activities

  • Agriculture
  • Trade
  • Hunting
  • Ironworking / smithing

The Portuguese reached Kongo in the late fifteenth century and later established strong influence there.

🌊 B. Swahili City-States

Bantu-speaking communities established settlements along the East African Indian Ocean coast.

Arab and Persian Muslim merchants later interacted with local communities.

Cultural diffusion:

The Swahili civilization developed through interaction among African Bantu, Arabic and Persian traditions.

Major Swahili city-states:

  • Mogadishu — Somalia
  • Malindi — Kenya
  • Mombasa — Kenya
  • Zanzibar — Tanzania
  • Kilwa — Tanzania
  • Brava
  • Pate
  • Pemba
  • Sofala

⭐ Kilwa

Kilwa became one of the most important commercial centers of East Africa.

It was particularly important in the gold and ivory trade.

Swahili city-states maintained commercial relations with Arabia, Persia, India and China.

🇿🇼 5.6 Southern Africa

🏛️ A. Great Zimbabwe

Great Zimbabwe was built by Shona-speaking Bantu peoples. They began establishing their power around AD 1000.

Great Zimbabwe became famous for its enormous stone walls, houses and tower.

Meaning of Zimbabwe:

The name is associated with a Shona term meaning “stone houses.”

Economic activities

  • Cattle keeping
  • Crop cultivation
  • Gold mining
  • Indian Ocean trade

Great Zimbabwe was part of a wealthy trading system connected to the East African coast.

👑 Mwene Mutapa

After about 1450 AD, Rozwi clan rulers of the Shona people established the empire known as Mwene Mutapa.

It became associated with gold and trade. Portuguese influence later increased in the region.

🐄 B. Khoi-Khoi

The Khoi-Khoi originally practiced hunting, but from around 1000 AD they increasingly practiced sheep and cattle herding.

By the fifteenth century they were well established in southern Africa.

🏹 C. San

The San traditionally lived through hunting and gathering. They used bows and poison-tipped arrows for hunting.

San languages belong to the Khoisan language group and are characterized by clicking sounds.

🤝 5.7 Africa's Intra- and Inter-continental Relations

📌 Intra-continental relations

“Intra” means within. Therefore, intra-continental relations refer to relationships among peoples and states within Africa.

🌍 Inter-continental relations

“Inter” means between. Inter-continental relations refer to relationships between Africa and other continents.

Major forms of relations

  • Trade
  • Migration
  • War
  • Diplomacy
  • Religious exchange
  • Cultural exchange
  • Movement of ideas and technologies

Trade across the Sahara strengthened relationships between North Africa and West Africa. The spread of Islam further connected different regions.

🐪 5.8 Trans-Saharan Trade

The Trans-Saharan trade connected peoples and states north and south of the Sahara Desert.

The use of camels made desert trade more practical and helped organize long-distance caravan routes.

🛣️ Major routes

Trade routes connected West and West-Central Africa with North and Northwest Africa. Other routes crossed the central Sahara and connected North Africa with Kanem.

📦 Major goods

Region Important goods
West Africa Gold
North Africa Salt
Various regions Textiles, animals and other goods
Slave trade Enslaved people became part of trade networks

📈 Effects of Trans-Saharan Trade

  • Increased wealth
  • Growth of cities
  • Growth of powerful states
  • Spread of Islam
  • Movement of ideas
  • Cultural interaction
  • Development of political systems
Important West African Empires connected with Trans-Saharan trade:

Ghana, Mali and Songhay became powerful partly because they controlled important trade routes.

🌐 5.9 Early Contacts with the Outside World

Africa had contact with peoples outside the continent long before European colonialism.

🌍 Important outside contacts

  • Arabs
  • Greeks
  • Persians
  • Romans
  • Indians
  • Chinese
  • Spanish
  • Portuguese

🌊 East African Coast

The East African coast was especially accessible to maritime trade across the Indian Ocean.

African coastal communities traded with Arabia, Persia, India and China.

⛵ European exploration

From the fifteenth century, European powers, especially Portugal and Spain, became increasingly interested in African and international trade.

They sponsored voyages of exploration in order to gain access to trade routes, resources and markets.

⚠️ Consequences

  • Increased international trade
  • Cultural exchange
  • Economic changes
  • Political changes
  • Changes in African coastal societies
  • Eventually, development of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade
Important:

The textbook explains that Afro-European contact during the Age of Exploration contributed to the opening of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.
