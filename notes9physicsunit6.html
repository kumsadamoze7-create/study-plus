Unit 6 — Mechanical Oscillation and Sound Wave

📖 Complete Study Notes

📘 Unit 6 Introduction

Mechanical oscillation is a repeated back-and-forth motion about a fixed or equilibrium position.

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without permanently transferring the material of the medium.

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibrating objects and requires a material medium for propagation.

1. Mechanical Oscillation

An oscillation is a repeated motion of an object around its equilibrium or rest position.

Examples of oscillatory motion include:

  • A swinging pendulum
  • A vibrating guitar string
  • A mass attached to a spring
  • A vibrating tuning fork

2. Equilibrium Position

The equilibrium position is the position where an object would remain at rest if no disturbance acts on it.

During oscillation, an object moves to one side of the equilibrium position and then to the other side repeatedly.

3. Amplitude

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of an oscillating object from its equilibrium position.

Amplitude = Maximum displacement from equilibrium

Amplitude is usually represented by the symbol A.

A larger amplitude means the object moves farther from its equilibrium position.

4. Period

The period, represented by T, is the time required for one complete oscillation or one complete cycle.

T = Time / Number of oscillations

The SI unit of period is the second (s).

If 10 oscillations take 20 seconds:

T = 20 / 10

T = 2 s

5. Frequency

Frequency, represented by f, is the number of complete oscillations made per unit time.

f = Number of oscillations / Time

f = 1 / T

The SI unit of frequency is the Hertz (Hz).

If an object makes 20 oscillations in 10 seconds:

f = 20 / 10

f = 2 Hz

6. Relationship Between Period and Frequency

f = 1/T

T = 1/f

Period and frequency are inversely related.

When frequency increases, the period decreases. When frequency decreases, the period increases.

7. Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a small mass attached to a light string that is fixed at one end.

When the mass is displaced from its equilibrium position and released, it oscillates back and forth.

Important Terms

  • Bob: The mass attached to the string.
  • Length: Distance from the point of suspension to the center of the bob.
  • Equilibrium position: The central resting position.
  • Amplitude: Maximum displacement from equilibrium.

8. Factors Affecting the Period of a Pendulum

For a simple pendulum undergoing small oscillations, the period mainly depends on its length and gravitational acceleration.

T = 2π√(L/g)

Where:

  • T = period
  • L = length of pendulum
  • g = acceleration due to gravity
A longer pendulum has a longer period.

9. Spring-Mass Oscillation

A mass attached to a spring can oscillate back and forth when it is displaced from its equilibrium position.

The spring provides a restoring force that tends to bring the mass back toward equilibrium.

10. What is a Wave?

A wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium or space and transfers energy from one place to another.

Waves transfer energy, not matter as a whole, from one location to another.

11. Common Characteristics of Waves

  • Amplitude
  • Wavelength
  • Frequency
  • Period
  • Wave speed
  • Crest
  • Trough

12. Rest Position

The rest position is the undisturbed position of the particles in a medium when they are not vibrating.

13. Displacement

Displacement is the distance and direction of a point from its rest or equilibrium position.

14. Crest

The crest is the highest point of a transverse wave above the equilibrium position.

15. Trough

The trough is the lowest point of a transverse wave below the equilibrium position.

16. Wavelength

Wavelength, represented by λ, is the distance between two successive points that are in the same phase.

For example, it can be the distance from one crest to the next crest or from one trough to the next trough.

The SI unit of wavelength is the meter (m).

17. Wave Speed

Wave speed is the distance travelled by a wave per unit time.

v = fλ

Where:
v = wave speed
f = frequency
λ = wavelength
If:
f = 5 Hz
λ = 2 m

v = fλ
v = 5 × 2

v = 10 m/s

18. Important Wave Relationships

v = fλ

f = v/λ

λ = v/f

f = 1/T

19. Transverse Waves

In a transverse wave, the particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels.

Example:

Waves on a stretched string can be transverse waves.

20. Longitudinal Waves

In a longitudinal wave, the particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels.

Longitudinal waves have regions of:

  • Compression: Region where particles are close together.
  • Rarefaction: Region where particles are farther apart.

21. Transverse vs Longitudinal Waves

Transverse Longitudinal
Particles vibrate perpendicular to wave direction. Particles vibrate parallel to wave direction.
Has crests and troughs. Has compressions and rarefactions.
Example: string wave. Example: sound wave in air.

22. Mechanical Waves

A mechanical wave is a wave that requires a material medium to propagate.

Mechanical waves cannot travel through a vacuum.

Examples include:
  • Sound waves
  • Waves on a string
  • Water waves

23. Sound Wave

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibrating objects.

Sound needs a material medium such as a solid, liquid, or gas to travel.

Important: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to transmit the vibration.

24. Production of Sound

Sound is produced when an object vibrates.

Examples:
  • Vocal cords vibrate when we speak.
  • A guitar string vibrates when plucked.
  • A drum membrane vibrates when struck.
  • A tuning fork produces sound when it vibrates.

25. Sound in Different Media

Sound can travel through solids, liquids and gases.

In general, sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases.

Medium Sound propagation
Solid Generally fastest
Liquid Intermediate
Gas Generally slowest

26. Speed of Sound

The speed of sound depends on the medium through which it travels and, in gases, on temperature.

For air, the speed of sound generally increases as temperature increases.

At approximately room temperature, the speed of sound in air is around 343 m/s, although it varies with temperature and conditions.

27. Properties of Sound

Frequency

Frequency determines the pitch of a sound.

Amplitude

Amplitude is related to the loudness or intensity of sound. Greater amplitude generally produces a louder sound.

Pitch

Pitch describes how high or low a sound seems. Higher frequency means higher pitch.

Loudness

Loudness describes how strong or weak a sound appears to a listener and is related to the amplitude of the vibration.

28. Audible Sound

For a typical healthy human ear, audible sound is approximately in the frequency range of:

20 Hz to 20,000 Hz

29. Infrasound

Sound with a frequency below approximately 20 Hz is called infrasound.

Humans normally cannot hear infrasound.

30. Ultrasound

Sound with a frequency above approximately 20,000 Hz is called ultrasound.

Humans normally cannot hear ultrasound.

Ultrasound is used in applications such as medical imaging and industrial testing.

31. Superposition of Waves

When two or more waves meet at the same place, their displacements combine according to the principle of superposition.

The resulting wave depends on how the waves interact.

32. Interference

Interference occurs when waves overlap and combine.

Constructive Interference

When waves combine so that their amplitudes reinforce each other, the resulting amplitude becomes larger.

Destructive Interference

When waves combine so that their displacements oppose each other, the resulting amplitude becomes smaller.

33. Waves Transfer Energy

A major function of waves is the transfer of energy from one location to another.

A wave can transfer energy without transporting the medium itself from one place to another.
