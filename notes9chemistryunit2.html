📌 Introduction

Measurement and scientific method are fundamental parts of chemistry. Chemists use measurements to identify substances, compare properties, perform experiments, analyze results and solve scientific problems.

A good scientific investigation requires accurate measurements, appropriate instruments, careful observations and proper recording of experimental results.

2.1 Measurement

Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown quantity with an accepted standard quantity.

Every measurement normally consists of two parts:

  • A numerical value
  • A unit
Example:

The length of a table is 2 m.

2 = numerical value

m = unit

Why is Measurement Important in Chemistry?

  • To determine the amount of a substance.
  • To compare substances.
  • To calculate physical properties.
  • To conduct experiments.
  • To obtain reliable scientific results.

2.4 Derived Quantities

A derived quantity is a physical quantity obtained by combining two or more fundamental quantities mathematically.

Examples

  • Area
  • Volume
  • Speed
  • Density
Density = Mass / Volume
Speed = Distance / Time
Area = Length × Width
Volume = Length × Width × Height

2.6 Measurement of Volume

Volume is the amount of space occupied by a substance.

For a Regular Solid

V = Length × Width × Height

For a Liquid

A graduated cylinder can be used to measure liquid volume. The reading should normally be taken at eye level.

For an Irregular Solid

The displacement method can be used. The object is placed into a known volume of water and the change in water level gives the volume of the object.

Example:

Initial water volume = 50 mL

Final water volume = 70 mL

Volume of object = 70 − 50 = 20 mL

2.7 Measurement of Mass

Mass is the amount of matter contained in an object.

A balance is commonly used to measure mass.

Common Units

  • kilogram (kg)
  • gram (g)
  • milligram (mg)
1 kg = 1000 g
1 g = 1000 mg

2.8 Temperature

Temperature is a measure related to how hot or cold a substance is.

A thermometer is used to measure temperature.

Common Temperature Scales

  • Celsius (°C)
  • Kelvin (K)
K = °C + 273.15
Example:

25°C in Kelvin:

25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K

2.9 Unit Conversion

Length

  • 1 km = 1000 m
  • 1 m = 100 cm
  • 1 cm = 10 mm

Mass

  • 1 kg = 1000 g
  • 1 g = 1000 mg

Volume

  • 1 L = 1000 mL
  • 1 mL = 1 cm³
Example:

Convert 2.5 kg to grams.

2.5 × 1000 = 2500 g

2.10 Density

Density is the mass contained in a unit volume of a substance.

ρ = m / V

Where:

  • ρ = density
  • m = mass
  • V = volume

Common Units

  • g/cm³
  • g/mL
  • kg/m³
Example:

Mass = 100 g

Volume = 20 cm³

ρ = 100 / 20 = 5 g/cm³

2.11 Accuracy

Accuracy is the closeness of a measured value to the accepted or true value.

Accepted value = 50.0 g

Measured value = 50.1 g

The measured value is close to the accepted value, so it is accurate.

2.12 Precision

Precision refers to how close repeated measurements are to one another.

Measurements:

10.1 g, 10.1 g, 10.2 g, 10.1 g

These values are close to each other, so the measurements are precise.

Accuracy vs Precision

Accuracy Precision
Closeness to the true value Closeness of repeated measurements
Related to correctness Related to consistency

2.13 Significant Figures

Significant figures are the meaningful digits in a measurement. They give information about the precision of the measurement.

Examples

  • 25.4 → 3 significant figures
  • 5.20 → 3 significant figures
  • 0.0052 → 2 significant figures
  • 4500 → significance depends on how the number is written

Leading zeros are generally not counted as significant figures.

2.14 Scientific Notation

Scientific notation is used to express very large or very small numbers in a convenient form.

a × 10ⁿ

where a is normally between 1 and 10.

Examples

  • 3000 = 3 × 10³
  • 45000 = 4.5 × 10⁴
  • 0.0045 = 4.5 × 10⁻³

2.15 Scientific Method

The scientific method is a systematic process used by scientists to investigate questions, test ideas and solve scientific problems.

Main Steps

  1. Observation — noticing a phenomenon or problem.
  2. Question — identifying what needs to be investigated.
  3. Hypothesis — proposing a testable explanation.
  4. Experiment — testing the hypothesis.
  5. Data Collection — recording observations and measurements.
  6. Data Analysis — examining and interpreting the results.
  7. Conclusion — deciding whether the evidence supports the hypothesis.
  8. Communication — reporting the findings.

2.16 Variables in an Experiment

Independent Variable

The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed by the researcher.

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the factor measured or observed in response to the independent variable.

Controlled Variables

Controlled variables are factors kept constant during the experiment.

Example:

Suppose we investigate how temperature affects the time required for sugar to dissolve in water.

  • Independent variable → temperature
  • Dependent variable → dissolving time
  • Controlled variables → amount of sugar, amount of water, stirring method

2.17 Experimental Error

Experimental error is the difference between a measured value and an accepted or true value.

Experimental errors can result from measuring instruments, experimental conditions, human reading, environmental changes and limitations of the experimental procedure.

1. Random Error

Random error is an unpredictable variation that causes repeated measurements to differ from one another.

Example:

A student's measurements of the same object are:

10.1 cm, 10.2 cm, 10.0 cm and 10.1 cm.

The small unpredictable changes are random errors.

How to Reduce Random Error

  • Repeat the experiment several times.
  • Take the average of repeated measurements.
  • Use a more sensitive measuring instrument.

2. Systematic Error

Systematic error is an error that occurs consistently in the same direction. It can make measurements consistently too high or too low.

Example:

A balance that has not been properly calibrated adds 0.5 g to every measurement.

How to Reduce Systematic Error

  • Calibrate instruments.
  • Check instruments before use.
  • Use properly functioning equipment.
  • Follow correct experimental procedures.

3. Absolute Error

Absolute error is the absolute difference between the measured value and the accepted value.

Absolute Error = |Measured Value − Accepted Value|
Example:

Measured value = 10.5 g

Accepted value = 10.0 g

Absolute Error = |10.5 − 10.0| = 0.5 g

4. Percentage Error

Percentage error expresses the absolute error as a percentage of the accepted value.

Percentage Error = (|Measured Value − Accepted Value| / Accepted Value) × 100%
Example:

Measured value = 10.5 g

Accepted value = 10.0 g

Percentage Error = (0.5 / 10.0) × 100% = 5%

Therefore, the percentage error is 5%.

Random Error vs Systematic Error

Random Error Systematic Error
Unpredictable variation Consistent error
Measurements vary Measurements shift in one direction
Reduced by repeated measurements Reduced by calibration
Important:

Experimental error does not always mean that the scientist made a mistake. Some errors are caused by limitations of instruments or experimental conditions.

2.18 Data Collection

Data collection is the process of gathering information during an experiment or investigation.

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data are descriptive observations that are not mainly expressed as numbers.

Example: The solution changes from colourless to blue.

Quantitative Data

Quantitative data are numerical measurements or counts.

Example: The temperature is 25°C.

2.20 Laboratory Safety

  • Wear appropriate protective equipment.
  • Never taste laboratory chemicals.
  • Do not smell chemicals directly.
  • Read labels before using chemicals.
  • Keep chemicals away from food and drinks.
  • Follow laboratory instructions.
  • Report accidents immediately.
  • Handle glassware carefully.
  • Keep the laboratory clean.
  • Wash hands after experiments.
