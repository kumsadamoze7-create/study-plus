📘 Introduction

Measurement and scientific method are very important parts of chemistry. Chemists need accurate measurements to identify substances, compare materials, perform experiments and obtain reliable results.

The scientific method provides an organized way of investigating questions and solving scientific problems.

2.1 Measurement

What is Measurement?

Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown physical quantity with an agreed standard unit.

For example, when we say that the length of a table is 2 metres, we are comparing the length of the table with the standard unit metre.

Example:

If a pencil has a length of 15 cm:

Numerical value = 15
Unit = centimetre (cm)

Why is Measurement Important in Chemistry?

  • It helps determine the amount of a substance.
  • It allows scientists to compare experimental results.
  • It helps calculate density, concentration and other quantities.
  • It makes scientific results reproducible.
  • It is necessary for accurate laboratory experiments.

Physical Quantities

A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed using a numerical value and a unit.

Examples include length, mass, time, temperature and volume.

Types of Physical Quantities

1. Fundamental Quantities

Fundamental quantities are basic physical quantities that are not defined in terms of other physical quantities.

Quantity SI Unit Symbol
Length metre m
Mass kilogram kg
Time second s
Temperature kelvin K
Amount of substance mole mol
Electric current ampere A
Luminous intensity candela cd

2. Derived Quantities

Derived quantities are obtained by combining fundamental quantities mathematically.

Quantity SI Unit
Area m²
Volume m³
Speed m/s
Density kg/m³
Force newton (N)

SI means International System of Units. It is the internationally accepted system used by scientists.

Measurement of Length

The SI unit of length is the metre (m).

Common instruments used to measure length include:

  • Ruler
  • Metre rule
  • Measuring tape
  • Vernier caliper
  • Micrometer screw gauge
Example:

1 m = 100 cm

Therefore:

2.5 m = 250 cm

Measurement of Mass

Mass is the amount of matter in an object. The SI unit of mass is kilogram (kg).

In laboratories, mass is commonly measured using a balance.

  • Electronic balance
  • Triple-beam balance
  • Analytical balance
Example:

500 g = 0.5 kg

Measurement of Time

The SI unit of time is the second (s).

Common instruments include clocks, watches and stopwatches.

Conversions:

1 minute = 60 seconds

1 hour = 3600 seconds

Measurement of Temperature

Temperature indicates how hot or cold a substance is.

The SI unit is kelvin (K), while Celsius (°C) is also commonly used.

Thermometer

A thermometer is an instrument used to measure temperature.

K = °C + 273.15
Example:

25°C ≈ 298.15 K

Measurement of Volume

Volume is the amount of space occupied by a substance.

The SI unit of volume is cubic metre (m³). In laboratories, litre (L) and millilitre (mL) are commonly used.

For Regular Solids

Volume = length × width × height
Example:

A box has:

Length = 5 cm
Width = 2 cm
Height = 3 cm

Volume = 5 × 2 × 3 = 30 cm³

For Liquids

A graduated cylinder or measuring cylinder can be used.

For Irregular Solids

The water displacement method can be used.

Initial water level = 40 mL

Final water level = 65 mL

Volume of object = 65 − 40 = 25 mL

Density

Density is the mass contained in a unit volume of a substance.

Density = Mass / Volume

ρ = m / V
Example:

Mass = 200 g

Volume = 50 cm³

Density = 200 ÷ 50

Density = 4 g/cm³

Density is a useful physical property for identifying and comparing substances.

Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the accepted or true value.

Precision

Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to one another.

Important:

A measurement can be precise but not accurate. It can also be accurate but not highly precise.

Significant Figures

Significant figures are the digits in a measurement that carry meaningful information about its precision.

Examples

  • 25.4 has 3 significant figures.
  • 0.0052 has 2 significant figures.
  • 120.0 has 4 significant figures.
  • 3.00 has 3 significant figures.
Remember:

Leading zeros are generally not significant. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.

Scientific Notation

Scientific notation is a convenient way of writing very large or very small numbers.

a × 10ⁿ

where 1 ≤ a < 10.

Examples:

3000 = 3 × 10³

0.0045 = 4.5 × 10⁻³

250000 = 2.5 × 10⁵

Unit Conversion

Unit conversion changes a measurement from one unit to another without changing the actual quantity.

Example 1:

3 m = 300 cm

because:

1 m = 100 cm

Example 2:

2500 mg = 2.5 g

because:

1000 mg = 1 g

🔬 2.2 Scientific Method

The scientific method is a systematic process used by scientists to investigate questions, solve problems and develop explanations based on evidence.

Main Steps

  1. Observation
  2. Question or problem
  3. Hypothesis
  4. Experiment
  5. Data collection
  6. Data analysis
  7. Conclusion
  8. Communication of results

Observation

Observation is the process of gathering information using the senses or scientific instruments.

Observations may be qualitative or quantitative.

Qualitative Observation

Describes qualities without numerical measurements.

Example: The solution is blue.

Quantitative Observation

Includes numerical measurements.

Example: The temperature is 25°C.

Scientific Question / Problem

After making observations, a scientist identifies a question or problem that needs investigation.

Example:

Does increasing the temperature affect the rate at which sugar dissolves in water?

Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation or prediction about a scientific question.

Example:

"If the temperature of water increases, then sugar will dissolve faster."

A good hypothesis must be testable through investigation.

Variables

Independent Variable

The independent variable is the factor that the researcher deliberately changes.

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the factor that is measured or observed.

Controlled Variables

Controlled variables are factors kept constant so that they do not affect the result.

Example:

Experiment: Effect of temperature on dissolving sugar.

Independent variable: Temperature

Dependent variable: Time needed for sugar to dissolve

Controlled variables: Amount of sugar, amount of water, type of sugar and stirring conditions.

Experiment

An experiment is a controlled investigation performed to test a hypothesis.

A good experiment should be carefully planned and repeated when appropriate.

Data Collection

Data are pieces of information collected during an investigation.

Types of Data

  • Qualitative data
  • Quantitative data

Data can be organized using tables, graphs and charts.

Data Analysis

Data analysis involves examining collected data to identify patterns, relationships and trends.

Scientists may use calculations, tables, graphs and statistical methods.

Conclusion

A conclusion states what was learned from the experiment and whether the evidence supports or does not support the hypothesis.

Important:

A hypothesis is not simply "proved true" because of one experiment. Scientists use evidence to determine whether the results support it.

Communication of Results

Scientists communicate their findings so that other scientists can understand, evaluate and reproduce the work.

Results can be communicated through reports, tables, graphs, presentations and scientific publications.

🧤 2.3 Laboratory Safety

Laboratory safety is essential because chemicals, glassware, heat and laboratory equipment can cause injury if they are used incorrectly.

Important Safety Rules

  • Wear appropriate protective equipment.
  • Follow the teacher's or laboratory instructions.
  • Do not taste chemicals.
  • Do not smell chemicals directly.
  • Keep chemicals away from food and drinks.
  • Handle glassware carefully.
  • Keep the working area clean.
  • Report accidents immediately.
  • Read chemical labels before use.
  • Dispose of chemicals according to laboratory instructions.

⚠️ Chemical Safety Symbols

Hazard symbols provide information about possible dangers associated with chemicals.

  • 🔥 Flammable — can catch fire easily.
  • ☠️ Toxic — can cause serious harm or death.
  • 🧪 Corrosive — can damage skin, eyes or materials.
  • 💥 Explosive — may explode under certain conditions.
  • ⚠️ Irritant/Harmful — may cause irritation or other harm.

📉 2.4 Experimental Errors

Experimental error is the difference between a measured value and the accepted or true value.

Random Error

Random errors vary unpredictably from one measurement to another.

Repeating measurements and taking an average can help reduce the effect of random error.

Systematic Error

Systematic error tends to produce measurements that are consistently too high or too low.

It may result from a poorly calibrated instrument or an incorrect experimental procedure.

Random Error vs Systematic Error

Random Error Systematic Error
Changes unpredictably Usually occurs in a consistent direction
Can often be reduced by repeated measurements Requires finding and correcting the source
Causes scatter in results Can shift results away from the accepted value

Reliable Experimental Results

Reliable results are supported by careful measurement, appropriate equipment, controlled variables, repeated trials and correct analysis.

  • Use suitable measuring instruments.
  • Read instruments correctly.
  • Repeat measurements.
  • Record data immediately.
  • Control important variables.
  • Use appropriate units.
  • Analyze data carefully.
