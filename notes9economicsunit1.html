📖 Unit Introduction

Economics is an important social science that helps us understand how people, households, businesses and governments make choices when resources are limited but human wants are many.

Economics is useful in everyday life because people constantly make decisions about earning, spending, saving, producing, buying and using resources.

Main idea:
Economics studies how scarce resources can be allocated efficiently to satisfy human needs and wants as much as possible.

Why do we study Economics?

  • To understand how resources are allocated.
  • To understand how people make economic choices.
  • To understand production, consumption and distribution.
  • To understand economic problems such as poverty and unemployment.
  • To improve logical and analytical thinking.
  • To understand the behavior of households, firms and government.
  • To make better economic decisions in everyday life.

1.1 Meaning and Nature of Economics

Meaning of Economics

The word economics comes from the ancient Greek word oikonomia, which refers to the management of a household or family.

Today economics is a branch of the social sciences. It studies how people and society make choices concerning the use of limited resources to satisfy human needs and wants.

Definition:
Economics is a social science concerned with the efficient allocation of scarce resources to satisfy human needs and wants.

Important ideas contained in the definition

  1. Human needs and wants are many: People continuously want food, clothing, housing, education, transport, entertainment and many other goods and services.
  2. Resources are scarce: The resources used to produce goods and services are limited relative to human wants.
  3. Choice is necessary: Because resources are limited, people must choose among different alternatives.
  4. Allocation is necessary: Available resources must be distributed among competing uses.
  5. Efficiency is important: Resources should be used carefully so that waste is minimized and satisfaction is increased.

Human Needs and Wants

A need is something essential for basic life and well-being, such as food, water, shelter and basic clothing.

A want is something people desire but can live without. Examples include expensive phones, entertainment, luxury clothing and certain types of travel.

Example:
A student needs food and basic school materials. The student may also want a very expensive smartphone. Because income is limited, the student must make a choice.

Scarcity

Scarcity means that available resources are insufficient to satisfy all human wants at the same time.

Scarcity does not necessarily mean that something is completely absent. It means that it is limited compared with the amount people want.

Example:
A family may have 1,000 birr available for several purposes. It may want to buy food, clothes, books and other items. Since the money cannot satisfy every want at once, the family must choose.

Choice

Choice means selecting one alternative from several available alternatives.

Choice is necessary because scarcity prevents people from having everything they want.

Economic Theory

An economic theory is an organized explanation of economic relationships or behavior. It helps economists understand, explain and predict economic events.

Efficient Allocation

Efficient allocation means using available resources in a way that produces the greatest possible benefit while avoiding unnecessary waste.

If a farmer has limited land, labor and money, efficient allocation means deciding how to use these resources so that the farmer gets the best possible result.

Nature of Economics

Economics is generally described as both a science and an art.

Economics as a Science

Science develops systematic and organized knowledge by observing facts, identifying relationships, analyzing evidence and drawing conclusions.

Economics uses observation, data collection, analysis and reasoning to explain economic relationships and make predictions. Therefore, economics has the characteristics of a science.

  • It studies facts and relationships systematically.
  • It uses observation and analysis.
  • It develops theories and principles.
  • It can be used to explain and predict economic behavior.

Economics as an Art

Art refers to the practical application of knowledge, principles and techniques to achieve a particular objective.

Economics provides principles and methods that can help society deal with practical problems such as unemployment, poverty, inflation and inefficient use of resources.

Remember:
Science helps us understand economic relationships, while art involves applying economic knowledge to practical economic problems.

1.2 Branches of Economics

Economics has two major branches: Microeconomics and Macroeconomics.

Microeconomics

Microeconomics studies individual economic units such as individual consumers, households, firms and particular markets.

Micro = small.
Think of microeconomics as studying individual parts of the economy.

Examples of Microeconomics

  • The price of bread in a particular market.
  • The income of one household.
  • The production decision of one firm.
  • The demand for a particular product.
  • The cost of producing a particular good.
  • The saving decision of an individual.

Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole. It focuses on large or aggregate economic variables.

Macro = large.
Think of macroeconomics as studying the economy as a whole.

Examples of Macroeconomics

  • National income.
  • Gross Domestic Product (GDP).
  • Overall unemployment.
  • General price level and inflation.
  • Economic growth.
  • Balance of payments.
  • Overall employment.

Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

Microeconomics Macroeconomics
Studies individual economic units. Studies the economy as a whole.
Focuses on individual households and firms. Focuses on national and aggregate variables.
Studies individual markets. Studies the overall economy.
Example: price of one product. Example: general price level.
Example: income of one household. Example: national income.

Fundamental Problems of Microeconomics

Microeconomics is concerned with questions such as how consumers and firms make choices and how resources are allocated among different uses.

  • What goods and services should be produced?
  • How should firms produce goods?
  • How should consumers allocate their limited income?
  • How are prices and quantities determined in individual markets?

Fundamental Problems of Macroeconomics

  • How can an economy achieve high employment?
  • How can economic growth be achieved?
  • How can inflation be controlled?
  • How can economic stability be maintained?
  • How can national resources be used effectively?

1.3 Methods and Approaches of Studying Economics

Economists use different methods to study economic behavior. The two important methods introduced in this unit are:

  • Deductive method
  • Inductive method

Deductive Method

The deductive method starts from general principles and uses logical reasoning to reach conclusions about particular cases.

Deduction:
General → Particular
Simple example:
General idea: People usually demand less of a good when its price rises, other things remaining constant.

Particular case: If the price of a particular product rises, we may predict that its quantity demanded will decrease, assuming other relevant factors remain unchanged.

Inductive Method

The inductive method starts with particular observations and uses them to develop a general conclusion or principle.

Induction:
Particular → General
Simple example:
An economist observes many different markets and finds that quantity demanded often falls when price rises. From repeated observations, a general relationship may be developed.

Deductive vs Inductive

Deductive Inductive
Starts with general principles. Starts with particular observations.
Moves from general to particular. Moves from particular to general.
Uses logical reasoning. Uses observation and evidence.

Approaches of Studying Economics

Economics can also be studied using two important approaches: positive economics and normative economics.

Positive Economics

Positive economics deals with statements about what actually is or what can be examined using facts and evidence.

Example:
"The inflation rate increased this year." This statement can be checked using economic data.

Normative Economics

Normative economics deals with opinions, judgments or statements about what ought to be or what should happen.

Example:
"The government should reduce inflation." This contains a recommendation or value judgment.

Positive vs Normative Economics

Positive Economics Normative Economics
Deals with what is. Deals with what ought to be.
Based mainly on facts and evidence. Contains judgments or recommendations.
Can be tested against evidence. Depends partly on values and opinions.
Example: Unemployment increased. Example: Government should reduce unemployment.
Exam trick:
If a statement says "is, was, increased, decreased" and can be tested using data → think Positive.

If it says "should, ought to, must" and gives a recommendation → think Normative.

1.4 Decision-Making Units in an Economy

There are three major decision-making units in an economy:

  1. Households
  2. Business firms
  3. Government

1. Households

A household is a person or group of people living together who make economic decisions concerning consumption, saving and the use of resources.

Major roles of households

  • Consume goods and services.
  • Supply factors of production.
  • Receive income.
  • Save part of their income.
  • Make decisions about spending.

Factors of Production Supplied by Households

Factor Factor Income
Land Rent
Labour Wages
Capital Interest
Entrepreneurship Profit

2. Business Firms

A business firm is an organization that combines resources and inputs to produce goods and services for consumers and other users.

Major roles of firms

  • Produce goods and services.
  • Employ workers.
  • Purchase or hire resources.
  • Pay wages, rent, interest and other costs.
  • Sell goods and services.
  • Make production and investment decisions.
  • Seek to operate profitably.

3. Government

Government is another major decision-making unit. It makes economic decisions and provides services that are important for society.

Major roles of government

  • Provide public services.
  • Collect taxes and other government revenue.
  • Provide infrastructure.
  • Make and enforce economic laws.
  • Support economic stability.
  • Protect consumers and producers through appropriate regulations.
  • Provide services such as education and public health.

Interaction among the Three Units

Households, firms and government do not operate separately. They interact continuously in an economy.

Example:
A household supplies labour to a firm → the firm pays wages → the household uses income to buy goods and services → the firm receives revenue → government may collect taxes from households and firms and use the revenue to provide public services.
Unit Main Economic Activities
Households Consume, save, supply factors and receive income.
Business firms Produce, employ resources, sell goods and services.
Government Tax, regulate, provide public services and infrastructure.
