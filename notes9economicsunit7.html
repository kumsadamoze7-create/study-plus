📖 Unit Introduction

Macroeconomics deals with the economy as a whole. It studies the performance, structure, behaviour and decision-making of an economy as a whole.

Macroeconomists study variables such as national income, GDP, unemployment, inflation, output, consumption, saving, investment, international trade and international finance.

Macroeconomics = Study of the economy as a whole.

Major Macroeconomic Questions

  • How much does an economy produce?
  • What determines national income?
  • Why does unemployment occur?
  • Why do prices rise?
  • How can economic growth be achieved?
  • How can economic stability be maintained?

7.1 Definition of Macroeconomic Variables

Meaning of Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics is the study of economic behaviour and policies that influence the economy as a whole.

Macroeconomic variables are aggregate economic measures used to describe the performance and condition of an economy.

1. National Income

National income is broadly the aggregate monetary value of the final goods and services produced in an economy during a given period, usually one year.

2. Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the total value of currently produced final goods and services produced within the boundaries of a country during a given period, usually one year.

Important: GDP measures production within the territory of a country, regardless of who owns the factors of production.

Important Points about GDP

  • It measures current production.
  • It includes final goods and services.
  • It excludes intermediate goods to avoid double counting.
  • It measures production within the country's territory.
  • It uses market values of goods and services.

GDP Formula

GDP = Σ(P × Q)

P = Price of the final good or service

Q = Quantity of the final good or service

Example of GDP

Suppose a country produces:

Coffee = 10,000 units × 100 Birr
Wheat = 20,000 units × 50 Birr
Clothes = 5,000 units × 200 Birr

GDP is obtained by adding the market value of the final goods and services.

3. Gross National Product (GNP)

Gross National Product (GNP) is the total value of goods and services produced by domestically owned factors of production during a given period, usually one year, regardless of their geographical location.

GNP = GDP + NFI

Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFI)

Net Factor Income from abroad is the difference between factor income received from abroad by citizens and factor income paid to foreigners.

NFI = Factor Income Received from Abroad − Factor Income Paid to Foreigners

Relationship Between GDP and GNP

Condition Relationship
NFI > 0 GNP > GDP
NFI < 0 GNP < GDP
NFI = 0 GNP = GDP
Example:

GDP = 500 billion Birr
NFI = 20 billion Birr

GNP = 500 + 20
GNP = 520 billion Birr

4. Per Capita Income (PCI)

Per Capita Income is the average income or output per person in a country.

PCI = GDP ÷ Total Population
If:
GDP = 1,000,000 Birr
Population = 10,000 people

PCI = 1,000,000 ÷ 10,000
PCI = 100 Birr per person

Per capita income can be used to help evaluate the average living standard of a country's population.

5. Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP is the monetary value of currently produced goods and services measured at current prices.

Nominal GDP can increase because prices increase, even when the actual quantity of production has not increased.

6. Real GDP

Real GDP measures the value of currently produced goods and services using constant prices. It excludes the effect of price changes.

Real GDP = (Nominal GDP ÷ Price Index) × 100
If:
Nominal GDP = 500
Price Index = 125

Real GDP = (500 ÷ 125) × 100
Real GDP = 400

Nominal GDP vs Real GDP

Nominal GDP Real GDP
Uses current prices Uses constant prices
Affected by price changes Removes the effect of price changes
Less suitable for comparing real output over time Better measure of changes in real output

7. Final Goods and Intermediate Goods

Final goods are goods purchased for final use and are not used up in producing another good during the same period.

Intermediate goods are goods that are completely used up in the production of other goods during the same period.

Example:

Flour purchased by a bakery to make bread → Intermediate good.
Bread purchased by a household for consumption → Final good.
Exam Tip: Intermediate goods are not separately included in GDP because doing so can cause double counting.

7.2 Macroeconomic Goals

Countries use macroeconomic policies to achieve desirable changes in output, income, employment, prices and other aggregate economic variables.

1. Economic Growth

Economic growth is a consistent increase in national income and the productive capacity of an economy.

Economic growth can lead to:
  • Higher production
  • Higher income
  • More employment opportunities
  • Improved living standards

2. Full Employment

Full employment means the maximum possible utilization of factors of production in the production process.

It does not mean that every person must have a job at every moment. Some temporary unemployment can still exist.

3. Stable Balance of Payments

Balance of payments records economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world.

A major macroeconomic goal is to maintain a stable external economic position.

4. Price Stability

Price stability means maintaining a stable general price level and avoiding long periods of inflation or deflation.

Price stability helps maintain the purchasing power of money and provides a more predictable environment for economic decisions.

5. Fair Distribution of Income and Wealth

Fair distribution means distributing income and wealth according to principles considered just and equitable.

7.3 Macroeconomic Problems

The economy does not always work smoothly. There can be fluctuations in economic activity, including recession, unemployment, inflation and external imbalances.

1. Recession

A recession is a period in which national income, output and employment fall significantly below their potential levels.

During a recession, productive resources may remain idle or underutilized.

2. Inflation

Inflation is a situation characterized by a sustained increase in the general price level.

Inflation → General price level rises continuously over time.

Causes of Inflation

  • Increase in aggregate demand
  • Increase in money supply
  • Increase in production costs
  • Supply shortages

Demand-Pull Inflation

Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand increases faster than the economy's ability to produce goods and services.

Too Much Money / Demand ↓ Too Few Goods ↓ Prices Rise ↓ Demand-Pull Inflation

Cost-Push Inflation

Cost-push inflation occurs when the cost of production increases and producers raise prices.

Higher Production Cost ↓ Higher Product Prices ↓ Cost-Push Inflation

Effects of Inflation

  • Reduces purchasing power of money.
  • Raises the cost of living.
  • Creates uncertainty.
  • Can reduce the real value of savings.
  • Can affect income distribution.

3. Deflation

Deflation is a sustained decrease in the general price level.

Inflation Deflation
General prices rise General prices fall
Purchasing power of money tends to fall Purchasing power of money tends to rise

4. Unemployment

Unemployment occurs when people who are willing and able to work cannot find jobs.

Labour Force

The labour force consists of people who are employed and people who are unemployed but actively available for work.

Unemployment Rate

Unemployment Rate = (Unemployed People ÷ Labour Force) × 100
If:
Unemployed people = 1,000
Labour force = 10,000

Unemployment rate
= (1,000 ÷ 10,000) × 100
= 10%

5. Frictional Unemployment

Frictional unemployment is temporary unemployment that occurs while people are changing jobs or searching for better jobs.

A worker leaves one job and spends two months searching for a better job.

6. Structural Unemployment

Structural unemployment occurs when the skills of workers do not match the skills demanded by available jobs.

A worker trained for an outdated technology may have difficulty finding a job when employers require new technical skills.

7. Cyclical Unemployment

Cyclical unemployment is associated with fluctuations in economic activity, especially during recessions.

During a recession, businesses may reduce production and lay off workers.

8. Disguised Unemployment

Disguised unemployment occurs when too many workers are engaged in work where some workers contribute little or nothing to total output.

For example, a small farm may have more workers than are actually needed. Removing some workers may not reduce total output.
Important: Disguised unemployment is sometimes called hidden unemployment. It can occur where there is a surplus of labour.

9. Trade Balance

Trade balance is the difference between receipts from exports of goods and expenditure on imports of goods.

Trade Balance = Exports of Goods − Imports of Goods

Trade Balance Surplus

A trade surplus occurs when the value of exports is greater than the value of imports.

Exports > Imports

Trade Balance Deficit

A trade deficit occurs when expenditure on imports is greater than receipts from exports.

Imports > Exports

10. Balance of Payments

Balance of payments is a statistical record of economic transactions between residents of a country and residents of the rest of the world during a given period.

It provides information about a country's international economic transactions, including exports, imports and financial flows.

Balance of Payments Disequilibrium

Although the credit and debit sides of a balance of payments account are recorded in a balanced accounting system, the underlying external position may be in surplus or deficit. This imbalance is called disequilibrium.

Situation Meaning
Surplus Credits exceed debits
Deficit Debits exceed credits

🔗 Relationship Between Growth, Inflation and Unemployment

Economic growth can increase production and employment when productive capacity is being utilized.

However, if aggregate demand grows faster than productive capacity, inflationary pressure can increase.

During economic downturns, production and employment may fall, leading to higher unemployment.

Economic Growth ↓ Higher Production ↓ More Employment

But if Demand grows too fast ↓ Inflationary Pressure

📝 Unit 7 Summary

  • Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole.
  • GDP measures final goods and services produced within a country's territory.
  • GNP measures production by domestically owned factors, regardless of location.
  • GNP = GDP + NFI.
  • Per capita income is obtained by dividing GDP by population.
  • Nominal GDP uses current prices.
  • Real GDP uses constant prices and removes the effect of price changes.
  • Macroeconomic goals include economic growth, full employment, stable balance of payments, price stability and fair distribution of income and wealth.
  • Macroeconomic problems include recession, inflation, unemployment and external imbalances.
  • Demand-pull inflation results from excessive aggregate demand.
  • Cost-push inflation results from increased production costs.
  • Deflation is a sustained decrease in the general price level.
  • Frictional unemployment is temporary job-search unemployment.
  • Structural unemployment results from skill mismatch.
  • Cyclical unemployment is associated with economic fluctuations.
  • Disguised unemployment occurs when there are surplus workers with little or no additional contribution to output.
  • Trade balance is exports of goods minus imports of goods.
  • A trade deficit occurs when imports exceed exports.
  • Balance of payments records international economic transactions.
