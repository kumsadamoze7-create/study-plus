5.1 Chemical Bonding

Definition:
A chemical bond is the attractive force that holds atoms or ions together to form molecules or compounds.

Atoms usually form chemical bonds because bonded atoms can reach a more stable electronic arrangement. The electrons involved in bonding are mainly the outermost electrons, called valence electrons.

Why do atoms form bonds?

Most atoms form bonds to achieve a more stable electron arrangement. For many main-group elements, stability is associated with having eight electrons in the outermost shell. This idea is called the octet rule.

Hydrogen and helium are exceptions because their first electron shell can hold only two electrons. This is related to the duet rule.

Example:
Sodium has one valence electron. It can lose this electron and form Na⁺, while chlorine can gain one electron and form Cl⁻. The opposite charges attract and form ionic bonding.

5.2 Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are electrons found in the outermost occupied energy level of an atom. They play an important role in chemical bonding.

5.4 Ionic Bonding

Ionic Bond:
An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

Ionic bonding usually occurs between a metal and a nonmetal. The metal loses one or more valence electrons and becomes a positively charged ion called a cation. The nonmetal gains electrons and becomes a negatively charged ion called an anion.

Cation

A cation is a positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Anion

An anion is a negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Formation of Sodium Chloride

Sodium has one valence electron while chlorine has seven. Sodium transfers one electron to chlorine.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions attract each other because they have opposite charges. This attraction forms an ionic bond.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • Usually high melting points.
  • Usually high boiling points.
  • Many are crystalline solids.
  • They are often hard but brittle.
  • Many dissolve in water.
  • They conduct electricity when molten.
  • They can conduct electricity when dissolved in water.
  • Solid ionic compounds generally do not conduct electricity because their ions cannot move freely.

5.5 Covalent Bonding

Covalent Bond:
A covalent bond is a chemical bond formed when atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.

Covalent bonding commonly occurs between nonmetal atoms. Instead of transferring electrons completely, the atoms share electrons so that they can obtain a more stable electron arrangement.

Single Covalent Bond

A single covalent bond is formed when two atoms share one pair of electrons.

H — H

The H₂ molecule contains one shared pair of electrons.

Double Covalent Bond

A double bond occurs when two atoms share two pairs of electrons.

O = O

Oxygen gas, O₂, contains a double covalent bond.

Triple Covalent Bond

A triple bond occurs when two atoms share three pairs of electrons.

N ≡ N

Nitrogen gas, N₂, contains a triple covalent bond.

5.5.1 Coordinate Covalent Bond

Coordinate Covalent Bond:
A coordinate covalent bond, also called a dative covalent bond, is a covalent bond in which both electrons in the shared pair are donated by the same atom.

In an ordinary covalent bond, each atom usually contributes one electron to the shared pair. In a coordinate covalent bond, however, one atom provides both electrons of the shared pair, while the other atom accepts the electron pair.

How does a coordinate covalent bond form?

  1. One atom must have a lone pair of electrons.
  2. The other atom or ion must be able to accept an electron pair.
  3. The atom with the lone pair donates both electrons.
  4. The donated electron pair becomes a shared pair.
  5. A coordinate covalent bond is formed.

Example 1 — Ammonium Ion

Formation of NH₄⁺

Ammonia (NH₃) contains a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen. The hydrogen ion (H⁺) does not have an electron pair to contribute. Therefore, nitrogen donates its lone pair to H⁺.

NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺

The nitrogen atom supplies both electrons for the newly formed N—H bond.

Example 2 — Hydronium Ion

Formation of H₃O⁺

Water (H₂O) contains lone pairs of electrons on oxygen. The oxygen atom donates one lone pair to H⁺.

H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺

The newly formed O—H bond is a coordinate covalent bond.

Electron Pair Donor and Acceptor

Part Meaning
Electron-pair donor The atom or ion that provides both electrons.
Electron-pair acceptor The atom or ion that accepts the electron pair.
Shared pair The two electrons involved in the bond.

How is it represented?

A coordinate covalent bond can be represented using an arrow. The arrow points from the donor toward the acceptor.

N → H

O → H

Ordinary Covalent vs Coordinate Covalent

Feature Ordinary Covalent Coordinate Covalent
Electron sharing Electrons are shared. Electrons are shared.
Source of electrons Usually one electron from each atom. Both electrons come from one atom.
Donor Both atoms contribute. One atom contributes both electrons.
Example H—H NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
Important:
A coordinate covalent bond is still a covalent bond because the electrons are shared after the bond forms. The special feature is that both electrons originally come from the same atom.

Properties of Covalent Substances

  • Many have relatively low melting points.
  • Many have relatively low boiling points.
  • Many exist as gases or liquids at room temperature.
  • Many molecular covalent substances do not conduct electricity.
  • Some are soluble in organic solvents.
  • The properties depend strongly on molecular structure.

5.6 Metallic Bonding

Metallic Bond:
Metallic bonding is the attraction between positive metal ions and delocalized electrons within a metal structure.

In metals, outer electrons are not strongly associated with one particular atom. They can move through the metal structure. These mobile electrons help explain many characteristic properties of metals.

Properties of Metals

  • Good electrical conductivity
  • Good thermal conductivity
  • Malleability
  • Ductility
  • Metallic lustre
  • Usually high strength

Malleability

Malleability is the ability of a material to be hammered or pressed into sheets without breaking.

Ductility

Ductility is the ability of a material to be drawn into wires.

5.7 Lewis Symbols and Lewis Structures

Lewis symbols represent an element's symbol surrounded by dots showing its valence electrons.

Examples:

Na → 1 valence electron
Mg → 2 valence electrons
O → 6 valence electrons
Cl → 7 valence electrons

Lewis Structures

Lewis structures show valence electrons and the way atoms share or transfer electrons during bonding.

Octet Rule

The octet rule states that atoms often tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to obtain eight electrons in their valence shell.

Duet Rule

Hydrogen follows the duet rule. It becomes stable when it has two electrons in its first shell.

5.8 Electronegativity and Chemical Bonds

Electronegativity:
Electronegativity is the ability of an atom in a chemical bond to attract the shared electrons toward itself.

The difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms helps us understand the nature of a chemical bond.

Non-polar Covalent Bond

When two atoms have the same or very similar electronegativities, the shared electrons are distributed relatively equally.

H₂ is an example of a non-polar covalent molecule.

Polar Covalent Bond

A polar covalent bond occurs when electrons are shared unequally because one atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly.

The O—H bonds in water are polar covalent bonds because oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen.

Ionic Character

As the difference in electronegativity becomes larger, the bond generally has greater ionic character.

5.10 Important Differences

Ionic Bond

Electron transfer → ions → electrostatic attraction.

Covalent Bond

Electron sharing → molecules or covalent structures.

Coordinate Covalent Bond

Both electrons in the shared pair are donated by one atom.

Metallic Bond

Metal ions + mobile/delocalized electrons.

Remember:

Ionic bonding is mainly associated with electron transfer.
Covalent bonding is mainly associated with electron sharing.
Coordinate covalent bonding is a special type of covalent bonding where both electrons in the shared pair come from one atom.
Metallic bonding involves metal ions and delocalized electrons.
