📘 Unit 2 Introduction

The Earth contains millions of different organisms. They differ in their structure, size, shape, habitat, nutrition, reproduction and many other characteristics.

Because there are so many organisms, scientists need a systematic way of identifying, naming and grouping them. This process is called classification.

Classification:

The systematic arrangement of organisms into groups according to their similarities and differences.

1️⃣ Characteristics of Living Organisms

Although organisms are very different from each other, they share important characteristics that distinguish living things from non-living things.

1. Cellular Organization

All living organisms are made up of one or more cells. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

  • Unicellular organisms consist of one cell.
  • Multicellular organisms consist of many cells.

2. Nutrition

Living organisms need nutrients and energy to carry out life processes.

Plants can make their own food through photosynthesis, while animals obtain food by eating other organisms.

3. Respiration

Organisms release energy from food through cellular respiration.

4. Growth

Living organisms increase in size and/or cell number.

5. Movement

Living organisms show movement. Plants may show slow movements or growth movements, while animals can usually move from one place to another.

6. Response to Stimuli

Living organisms detect and respond to changes in their environment.

Examples include light, temperature, sound and touch.

7. Reproduction

Organisms produce new individuals of their own kind.

Reproduction may be sexual or asexual.

8. Excretion

Organisms remove waste products produced by metabolic activities.

9. Adaptation

Organisms have characteristics that help them survive in their environments.

2️⃣ Classification of Organisms

Classification makes the study of organisms easier by placing organisms with similar characteristics into groups.

Why do we classify organisms?

  • To make identification easier.
  • To organize the huge diversity of organisms.
  • To make communication among scientists easier.
  • To understand relationships between organisms.
  • To study organisms systematically.
  • To avoid confusion caused by different common names.

3️⃣ Taxonomy

Taxonomy is the branch of biology concerned with the identification, naming and classification of organisms.

Three major activities of taxonomy

  1. Identification — determining what organism is being studied.
  2. Naming — giving organisms scientific names.
  3. Classification — placing organisms into appropriate groups.

👨‍🔬 4️⃣ Important Scientists in Classification

1. Aristotle

Aristotle was an ancient Greek philosopher and naturalist. He made one of the earliest attempts to classify living organisms.

His work

  • He classified animals mainly according to their observable characteristics.
  • He divided animals into groups such as those with blood and those without blood.
  • His classification was based mainly on external features and habitat.
Remember: Aristotle is one of the earliest scientists associated with biological classification.

2. Theophrastus

Theophrastus was a Greek philosopher and student of Aristotle. He made major contributions to the study and classification of plants.

His work

  • Studied many kinds of plants.
  • Described plant structures and characteristics.
  • Grouped plants according to their similarities.
Important:

Theophrastus is often called the Father of Botany.

3. Carolus Linnaeus

Carolus Linnaeus was a Swedish scientist who made major contributions to modern biological classification.

His major work

  • Developed a systematic classification system.
  • Established the widespread use of binomial nomenclature.
  • Organized organisms into hierarchical groups.

Binomial Nomenclature

Binomial nomenclature is the scientific naming system in which each species is given a two-part name.

Two parts:

Genus + specific epithet

Example: Homo sapiens

Rules

  • Genus begins with a capital letter.
  • The specific epithet begins with a small letter.
  • The scientific name is normally written in italics.
  • When handwritten, the two words are underlined separately.

4. Ernst Haeckel

Ernst Haeckel was a German biologist who contributed to the development of kingdom-level classification.

His work

  • Proposed a three-kingdom classification system.
  • Recognized the need for a separate group for organisms that did not fit well into plants or animals.
  • Developed the kingdom Protista concept.

5. Herbert Copeland

Herbert Copeland was an American biologist who proposed a four-kingdom classification system.

His work

  • Recognized the major difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms.
  • Placed prokaryotes in a separate kingdom.
  • Proposed a four-kingdom system.

6. Robert H. Whittaker ⭐

Robert H. Whittaker was an American ecologist who proposed the famous five-kingdom classification system in 1969.

His Five Kingdoms

  1. Monera
  2. Protista
  3. Fungi
  4. Plantae
  5. Animalia

Basis of Whittaker's classification

  • Cell type
  • Cellular organization
  • Mode of nutrition
  • Reproduction
  • Evolutionary relationships

🧬 5️⃣ Five Kingdom Classification

The five-kingdom system proposed by Whittaker divides living organisms into five major kingdoms.

Kingdom Cell Type Organization Nutrition
Monera Prokaryotic Mostly unicellular Autotrophic / Heterotrophic
Protista Eukaryotic Mostly unicellular Autotrophic / Heterotrophic
Fungi Eukaryotic Uni / Multicellular Heterotrophic
Plantae Eukaryotic Multicellular Mainly autotrophic
Animalia Eukaryotic Multicellular Heterotrophic

🔵 Kingdom Monera

Monera contains organisms with prokaryotic cells.

Characteristics

  • Prokaryotic organisms.
  • Usually unicellular.
  • No true nucleus.
  • DNA is located in the nucleoid region.
  • No membrane-bound organelles.
  • Most have a cell wall.
  • Some have flagella for movement.
  • Reproduction commonly occurs by binary fission.
  • They may be autotrophic or heterotrophic.

Examples

  • Bacteria
  • Cyanobacteria
Exam point:

Monera = Prokaryotic

🟣 Kingdom Protista

Protista contains mostly unicellular eukaryotic organisms.

Characteristics

  • Eukaryotic.
  • Mostly unicellular.
  • Some are colonial or simple multicellular forms.
  • True nucleus is present.
  • Membrane-bound organelles are present.
  • Some are autotrophic.
  • Some are heterotrophic.
  • Some move using cilia, flagella or pseudopodia.

Examples

  • Amoeba
  • Paramecium
  • Euglena
Exam point:

Protista = mostly unicellular + eukaryotic.

🟤 Kingdom Fungi

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms that obtain nutrients from organic matter.

Characteristics

  • Eukaryotic.
  • Unicellular or multicellular.
  • Heterotrophic.
  • Do not perform photosynthesis.
  • Cell wall mainly contains chitin.
  • Many multicellular fungi consist of hyphae.
  • A mass of hyphae is called a mycelium.
  • Many reproduce by spores.
  • They digest food externally and absorb nutrients.

Examples

  • Mushrooms
  • Yeast
  • Moulds
Exam point:

Fungi = eukaryotic + heterotrophic + chitin cell wall.

🟢 Kingdom Plantae

Plantae contains multicellular eukaryotic organisms that are mainly photosynthetic.

Characteristics

  • Multicellular.
  • Eukaryotic.
  • Usually autotrophic.
  • Contain chlorophyll in photosynthetic cells.
  • Cell wall mainly contains cellulose.
  • Store food mainly as starch.
  • Usually do not move from place to place.
  • Can reproduce sexually and/or asexually.

Major groups

Bryophytes

Non-vascular plants such as mosses.

Gymnosperms

Seed-producing plants with seeds that are not enclosed within fruits.

Angiosperms

Flowering plants whose seeds are enclosed within fruits.

Examples

  • Maize
  • Wheat
  • Teff
  • Rose
  • Eucalyptus

🔴 Kingdom Animalia

Animalia contains multicellular eukaryotic organisms that obtain food by consuming other organisms.

Characteristics

  • Multicellular.
  • Eukaryotic.
  • Heterotrophic.
  • Cells have no cell wall.
  • Most animals can move actively.
  • Most reproduce sexually.
  • Have specialized tissues and organs.

Two Major Groups

Invertebrates

Animals without a backbone.

  • Sponges
  • Flatworms
  • Roundworms
  • Annelids
  • Molluscs
  • Arthropods

Vertebrates

Animals with a backbone.

  • Fish
  • Amphibians
  • Reptiles
  • Birds
  • Mammals
Exam point:

Animalia = multicellular + eukaryotic + heterotrophic + no cell wall.

7️⃣ Taxonomic Hierarchy

Organisms are classified into a series of hierarchical categories.

Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

The groups become smaller and more specific as we move from kingdom toward species.

Species

Species is the basic unit of biological classification. A species generally consists of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

8️⃣ Scientific Names

Common names can vary between countries and languages. Scientific names provide a universal way of identifying species.

Example

Homo sapiens

  • Homo = Genus
  • sapiens = specific epithet

🧠 🔟 Key Terms

  • Classification: Grouping organisms according to similarities and differences.
  • Taxonomy: Science of identification, naming and classification.
  • Species: Basic unit of biological classification.
  • Prokaryote: Organism whose cell lacks a true nucleus.
  • Eukaryote: Organism whose cell contains a true nucleus.
  • Autotroph: Organism capable of producing its own food.
  • Heterotroph: Organism that obtains food from other sources.
  • Binomial nomenclature: Two-part scientific naming system.
  • Kingdom: A major taxonomic category.
