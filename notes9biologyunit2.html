Unit 2 Introduction

Living organisms are found almost everywhere on Earth. They include plants, animals, fungi, bacteria and many other forms of life. Although organisms are very different from one another, they share certain characteristics that distinguish living things from non-living things.

Because there are millions of different organisms, scientists need a system for identifying, naming and grouping them. This process is called classification.

In this unit we study:
  • Characteristics of living organisms
  • Classification of organisms
  • Taxonomy
  • Binomial nomenclature
  • Taxonomic hierarchy
  • Kingdoms of organisms
  • Viruses and their characteristics

2.1 Characteristics of Living Organisms

Living organisms have several characteristics that distinguish them from non-living things. No single characteristic alone is enough to define life; organisms are recognized by the combination of these characteristics.

1. Cellular Organization

All living organisms are made of one or more cells. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Some organisms consist of only one cell and are called unicellular organisms. Others consist of many cells and are called multicellular organisms.

Examples:
  • Bacteria are generally unicellular.
  • Humans are multicellular.
  • Most plants are multicellular.

2. Nutrition

Living organisms need nutrients and energy to carry out life processes.

Plants can produce their own food through photosynthesis and are therefore called autotrophs.

Animals cannot produce their own food and obtain nutrients by eating other organisms. They are generally called heterotrophs.

3. Respiration

Organisms release energy from food through cellular respiration. The energy produced is used for growth, movement, reproduction, repair and other life processes.

4. Growth

Living organisms increase in size or develop through their life cycle. Growth involves an increase in cell size, cell number, or both.

5. Reproduction

Reproduction is the process by which organisms produce new organisms of their own kind.

There are two major types of reproduction: sexual reproduction and asexual reproduction.

6. Excretion

Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste products from an organism.

Example: Humans remove carbon dioxide through breathing and nitrogenous wastes through urine.

7. Movement

Living organisms show movement. Animals can move their bodies from one place to another, while plants may show movements such as growth toward light.

8. Response to Stimuli

Living organisms detect and respond to changes in their environment. A change that causes a response is called a stimulus.

Example: A person quickly moves their hand away after touching a hot object.

9. Adaptation

Organisms have characteristics that help them survive in their environment. These characteristics are called adaptations.

10. Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal condition despite changes in the external environment.

Important characteristics:
  • Cellular organization
  • Nutrition
  • Respiration
  • Growth
  • Reproduction
  • Excretion
  • Movement
  • Response to stimuli
  • Adaptation
  • Homeostasis

2.2 Classification of Organisms

Classification is the process of grouping organisms according to their similarities and differences.

Scientists classify organisms to make it easier to identify, study and communicate information about them.

Why do we classify organisms?

  • To organize the enormous diversity of living organisms.
  • To make identification easier.
  • To understand relationships among organisms.
  • To avoid confusion caused by different common names.
  • To make scientific communication easier.

2.3 Taxonomy

Taxonomy is the branch of biology concerned with identifying, naming and classifying organisms.

Identification

Identification is determining the identity of an organism by examining its characteristics.

Naming

Scientists use standardized scientific names so that the same organism can be recognized internationally.

Classification

Classification places organisms into groups based on similarities, differences and evolutionary relationships.

Taxonomy includes:

Identification + Naming + Classification

2.4 Binomial Nomenclature

Binomial nomenclature is the scientific system of naming organisms using two names.

The system was developed and popularized by Carolus Linnaeus.

The two parts

  1. Genus
  2. Specific epithet

Together, the genus and specific epithet form the scientific name of an organism.

Example:

Homo sapiens

Homo = Genus
sapiens = Specific epithet

Rules for scientific names

  • The genus begins with a capital letter.
  • The specific epithet begins with a small letter.
  • The scientific name is usually written in italics.
  • When handwritten, the two words can be underlined separately.

2.5 Taxonomic Hierarchy

Taxonomic hierarchy is the arrangement of organisms into a series of groups from broad categories to more specific categories.

Major taxonomic ranks

Rank Meaning
Domain Very broad group of organisms
Kingdom Large group containing related organisms
Phylum Group of related classes
Class Group of related orders
Order Group of related families
Family Group of related genera
Genus Group of closely related species
Species Most specific major rank
Remember the order:

Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

As we move from Domain toward Species, the groups become smaller and organisms become more closely related.

2.6 Major Groups of Organisms

Organisms can be grouped according to characteristics such as cell type, number of cells, mode of nutrition and evolutionary relationships.

Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Organisms

One important distinction is between organisms with prokaryotic cells and those with eukaryotic cells.

Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells do not have a membrane-bound nucleus. Bacteria are examples of organisms with prokaryotic cells.

Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells have a membrane-bound nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.

Basic comparison:
Feature Prokaryotic Eukaryotic
Nucleus No membrane-bound nucleus Membrane-bound nucleus
Examples Bacteria Plants, animals, fungi

Bacteria

Bacteria are generally unicellular prokaryotic organisms. They can live in many different environments.

Some bacteria are useful, for example in food production and decomposition, while others can cause disease.

Protists

Protists are diverse eukaryotic organisms. Many are unicellular, although some are multicellular or colonial.

Fungi

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms that obtain nutrients by absorbing organic materials from their surroundings.

Examples include mushrooms, molds and yeasts.

Plants

Plants are multicellular eukaryotic organisms. Most plants contain chlorophyll and make food through photosynthesis.

Animals

Animals are multicellular eukaryotic organisms that obtain food by consuming other organisms or organic materials.

2.7 Viruses

Viruses are extremely small infectious agents. They consist mainly of genetic material surrounded by a protein coat.

Viruses cannot reproduce independently. They must enter a suitable host cell and use the host cell's machinery to make more viruses.

Structure of a Virus

  • Genetic material: DNA or RNA.
  • Capsid: Protein coat surrounding the genetic material.
  • Some viruses also have an additional outer envelope.

Examples of viral diseases

  • Influenza
  • Measles
  • HIV/AIDS
  • COVID-19
Important: Viruses are different from cellular organisms because they do not have a complete cellular structure and cannot reproduce independently.

  • Taxonomy deals with identification, naming and classification.
  • Binomial nomenclature gives organisms scientific names using two parts.

  • Viruses require host cells for reproduction.
