📘 Unit 4 Introduction

The periodic table is one of the most important tools in chemistry. It organizes elements in a systematic way so that elements with similar properties can be studied and compared easily.

As scientists discovered more elements, they needed a method for classifying them. The development of the periodic table passed through several important stages, including Döbereiner's Triads, Newlands' Law of Octaves, Mendeleev's periodic table and finally the modern periodic table.

Main idea: The modern periodic table arranges elements according to increasing atomic number and shows a periodic repetition of their physical and chemical properties.

4.1 Historical Development of Periodic Classification of Elements

When only a few elements were known, it was relatively easy to study them individually. As the number of known elements increased, scientists needed a systematic method of classification.

The purpose of classification was to group elements that have similar properties and to make it easier to predict the properties of elements that had not yet been studied.

Important historical attempts include:

  • Döbereiner's Triads
  • Newlands' Law of Octaves
  • Mendeleev's Periodic Table
  • The Modern Periodic Table

4.1.1 Döbereiner's Triads

Definition

Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner was a German chemist who attempted to classify elements into groups of three. These groups were called triads.

Triad: A group of three elements having similar chemical properties in which the atomic mass of the middle element is approximately related to the masses of the other two.

Example of a Triad

Lithium — Sodium — Potassium

Li    Na    K

These three elements have similar chemical properties. They are all alkali metals and tend to form +1 ions.

Another Example

A commonly discussed historical triad is:

Ca — Sr — Ba

Calcium, strontium and barium have similar properties and belong to Group 2 of the modern periodic table.

Importance

  • It was one of the first attempts to classify elements according to their properties.
  • It showed that some elements have systematic relationships.
  • It helped scientists move toward a periodic classification.

Limitations

  • Only a small number of elements could be arranged into triads.
  • Many known elements did not fit the triad system.
  • It could not provide a complete classification of all elements.

4.1.2 Newlands' Law of Octaves

John Newlands

John Newlands arranged elements in increasing order of atomic mass. He observed that the properties of some elements appeared to repeat after every eighth element.

Newlands' Law of Octaves:
When elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic mass, every eighth element shows similarities in properties, similar to the eighth note in music.

Why was it called Octaves?

In music, an octave contains eight notes. Newlands used this idea because he noticed a repeating pattern after every eight elements.

Example idea:

1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → 7 → 8
Similar properties were observed between some elements separated by seven elements.

Importance

  • It showed that element properties could repeat periodically.
  • It provided evidence for periodicity.
  • It contributed to the development of later periodic tables.

Limitations

  • The law worked reasonably well only for some lighter elements.
  • It did not work properly for heavier elements.
  • Newlands did not leave enough spaces for undiscovered elements.
  • Some elements with different properties were forced into the same group.

4.2 Mendeleev Classification of the Elements

Dmitri Ivanovich Mendeleev developed a much more successful classification of elements in 1869.

He arranged elements mainly according to increasing atomic mass and placed elements with similar chemical properties in the same vertical groups.

Major achievements of Mendeleev

  • He arranged known elements systematically.
  • He left gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered.
  • He predicted properties of some undiscovered elements.
  • His table helped scientists identify relationships between elements.

Why were gaps important?

Mendeleev believed that the gaps represented elements that had not yet been discovered. Instead of ignoring the gaps, he predicted their properties.

This was one of the strongest achievements of Mendeleev's periodic classification.

Limitations of Mendeleev's Table

  • It was based on atomic mass rather than atomic number.
  • The position of isotopes could not be explained properly.
  • Some pairs of elements did not fit perfectly according to atomic mass.
  • The position of hydrogen was uncertain.

4.2.1 Mendeleev's Periodic Law

The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses.

The important word in this law is periodic. It means that similar properties appear again at regular intervals when elements are arranged according to atomic mass.

Example

Elements belonging to the same group often showed similar chemical behavior. This allowed scientists to use the properties of known elements to understand other elements.

Important: Mendeleev's periodic law used atomic mass. The modern periodic law uses atomic number.

4.2.2 Periodicity

Definition

Periodicity is the regular repetition of similar physical and chemical properties of elements when they are arranged in increasing order of atomic number.

The reason for periodicity is closely related to the arrangement of electrons around the nucleus. Elements with similar valence-electron arrangements tend to have similar chemical properties.

Example

Element Electron configuration idea Group
Li 2,1 1
Na 2,8,1 1
K 2,8,8,1 1

All three have one electron in their outermost shell. Therefore, they show similar chemical behavior.

Key relationship:
Electronic configuration → Valence electrons → Chemical properties → Periodicity

4.3 The Modern Periodic Table

The modern periodic table is an organized arrangement of chemical elements in increasing order of atomic number.

The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Because each element has a unique number of protons, atomic number provides a reliable basis for classification.

Atomic number = Number of protons

4.3.1 Modern Periodic Law

The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

This law is different from Mendeleev's law because the modern law uses atomic number instead of atomic mass.

Why atomic number?

Atomic number determines the number of protons and, in a neutral atom, also determines the number of electrons. The arrangement of these electrons controls many chemical properties.

Atomic number → Number of electrons → Electron configuration → Chemical behavior

4.3.2 Groups and Periods

Groups

A group is a vertical column in the periodic table. The modern periodic table has 18 groups.

Elements in the same group often have similar chemical properties because they have similar outer-electron arrangements.

Examples

Group Name Example
1 Alkali metals Li, Na, K
2 Alkaline earth metals Mg, Ca
17 Halogens F, Cl, Br
18 Noble gases He, Ne, Ar

Periods

A period is a horizontal row in the periodic table. The modern periodic table has 7 periods.

For the main-group elements, the period number is related to the highest occupied principal energy level.

Example:
Sodium (Na) has electron arrangement 2,8,1. It has three occupied energy levels, so Na is in Period 3.

4.3.3 Periodic Classification of Elements — Blocks

The periodic table can be divided into four blocks according to the subshell into which the last electron is added.

1️⃣ S-Block

The last electron enters an s-subshell. The s-block mainly contains Groups 1 and 2.

Examples: Li, Na, K, Mg, Ca

Group 1 elements generally have one valence electron, while Group 2 elements generally have two valence electrons.

2️⃣ P-Block

The last electron enters a p-subshell. The p-block contains Groups 13–18.

Examples: B, C, N, O, F, Ne

The p-block contains metals, metalloids and non-metals, including the halogens and noble gases.

3️⃣ D-Block

The last electron enters a d-subshell. The d-block is located in the center of the periodic table and contains transition elements.

Examples: Fe, Cu, Ni, Zn

Many transition elements have variable oxidation states and form colored compounds. Several are also important catalysts.

4️⃣ F-Block

The f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides. It is normally shown separately at the bottom of the periodic table to keep the table compact.

  • Lanthanides
  • Actinides
Examples include Ce among lanthanides and U among actinides.

4.3.4 Representative Elements

Representative elements are generally the elements of the s-block and p-block.

Their chemical properties are strongly related to the number of electrons in their outermost shell.

Element Electron arrangement Valence electrons Group
Na 2,8,1 1 1
Mg 2,8,2 2 2
Cl 2,8,7 7 17
Ne 2,8 8 18

4.4 Major Trends in the Periodic Table

Periodic trends describe how atomic properties change as we move across periods or down groups.

Important trends include:

  • Atomic radius
  • Ionization energy
  • Electron affinity
  • Electronegativity
  • Metallic character
  • Non-metallic character

4.4.0 Atomic Radius

Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom. It is related to the distance between the nucleus and the outermost occupied electron shell.

Across a Period

Atomic radius generally decreases from left to right. The nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same main energy level. The nucleus therefore attracts the electrons more strongly.

Down a Group

Atomic radius generally increases from top to bottom because new electron shells are added.

Atomic radius:

Across → ⬇️
Down ↓ → ⬆️

4.4.1 Ionization Energy

Definition

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

Meaning

If an atom has high ionization energy, it holds its electrons strongly and it is difficult to remove an electron. If it has low ionization energy, an electron can be removed more easily.

Across a Period

Ionization energy generally increases from left to right because effective nuclear attraction increases.

Down a Group

Ionization energy generally decreases from top to bottom because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and is more shielded by inner electrons.

Ionization Energy:

Across → ⬆️
Down ↓ → ⬇️

Example

Alkali metals such as Na have relatively low ionization energies because their outer electron can be removed relatively easily. Noble gases generally have high ionization energies because their electron configurations are very stable.

4.4.2 Electron Affinity

Definition

Electron affinity describes the energy change associated with an isolated gaseous atom gaining an electron.

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

Why is it important?

Electron affinity helps us understand how strongly an atom tends to accept an additional electron. Elements that are close to completing their outer shell often have a strong tendency to gain electrons.

Example: Chlorine

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Chlorine has seven valence electrons. By gaining one electron it can achieve a stable outer-shell arrangement.

Periodic Trend

As a general trend, electron-accepting tendency becomes stronger across a period toward the right, especially among the halogens. Down a group, the tendency generally becomes weaker because the atomic radius increases and the incoming electron is farther from the nucleus.

Electron-accepting tendency:

Across → generally ⬆️
Down ↓ → generally ⬇️
Electron affinity has several important exceptions. Therefore, the trend should be understood as a general pattern rather than an absolute rule for every element.

4.4.3 Electronegativity

Definition

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electrons toward itself.

Example

In hydrogen chloride (HCl), chlorine attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen because chlorine has higher electronegativity.

H — Cl

Shared electrons are attracted more strongly toward Cl.

Across a Period

Electronegativity generally increases from left to right because effective nuclear attraction increases and atomic radius decreases.

Down a Group

Electronegativity generally decreases from top to bottom because atomic size increases and the bonding electrons are farther from the nucleus.

Electronegativity:

Across → ⬆️
Down ↓ → ⬇️

Fluorine

Fluorine has the highest electronegativity on the commonly used Pauling scale. It strongly attracts shared electrons in chemical bonds.

4.4.4 Metallic Character

Metallic character describes how strongly an element behaves like a metal. Metals tend to lose electrons and form positive ions.

Across a Period

Metallic character generally decreases from left to right.

Down a Group

Metallic character generally increases from top to bottom.

Metallic Character:

Across → ⬇️
Down ↓ → ⬆️

4.4.5 Non-Metallic Character

Non-metallic character describes how strongly an element behaves like a non-metal. Non-metals generally have a stronger tendency to gain or share electrons than metals.

Non-metallic character generally increases from left to right across a period and decreases down a group.

Non-metallic Character:

Across → ⬆️
Down ↓ → ⬇️

🧠 Easy Memory Trick

Atomic radius + Metallic character generally increase toward the bottom-left ↙️.

Ionization energy + Electronegativity generally increase toward the top-right ↗️.

Electron affinity has more exceptions, but electron-accepting tendency generally becomes stronger toward the right, especially for the halogens.

🔗 Relationship Between the Periodic Trends

The periodic properties are not independent. They are connected to atomic structure.

Atomic number ↓
Determines nuclear charge
↓
Affects electron arrangement
↓
Determines atomic radius and attraction
↓
Affects ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity
↓
Influences chemical properties

For example, when atomic size becomes smaller across a period, the nucleus can attract outer electrons more strongly. This helps explain why ionization energy and electronegativity generally increase across a period.
