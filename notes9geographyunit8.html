Unit 8 — Geographical Enquiry, Skills and Techniques

📖 Detailed Study Plus Notes

📚 Unit Introduction

Geography is not only about memorizing places and physical features. Geographers also need skills for collecting information, reading maps, measuring distances, locating places and interpreting geographic data.

Geographical enquiry is the process of asking questions, collecting geographic information, organizing and analyzing the information, and using the results to understand geographic problems.

Unit 8 contains:
  • Map and its basic components
  • Scale and scale conversion
  • Area and distance measurement
  • Position on maps
  • Grid references
  • Compass directions
  • Map sketching
  • Interpreting physical and human landscapes
  • Interpreting graphs, tables and diagrams

8.1 Geographical Enquiry

Geographical enquiry is a systematic process of asking geographic questions, collecting information, analyzing evidence and reaching conclusions.

Steps of Geographical Enquiry

  1. Identify a geographic problem or question.
  2. Collect relevant information.
  3. Organize the information.
  4. Analyze the information.
  5. Interpret the results.
  6. Draw conclusions.
  7. Communicate the findings.
Example:

A student wants to investigate why a nearby river is becoming polluted.

The student can:

  • Identify the problem.
  • Observe the river.
  • Collect information.
  • Interview local people.
  • Analyze the causes.
  • Suggest solutions.

8.2 Map and Its Basic Components

Map is a reduced, generalized and symbolized representation of all or part of the Earth's surface on a flat surface.

Why Maps Are Important

  • Show the location of places.
  • Show distribution of geographic features.
  • Help measure distances.
  • Help determine direction.
  • Help compare places.
  • Support planning and decision-making.
  • Help communicate geographic information.

Basic Components of a Map

  • Title
  • Scale
  • Direction / North arrow
  • Legend or key
  • Symbols
  • Grid or coordinates
  • Source
  • Frame / border

8.2.1 Map Title

The title tells the reader what the map represents.

Example:

"Population Density of Ethiopia"

This title tells us that the map represents the distribution of population density in Ethiopia.

8.3 Scale

Map scale is the relationship between a distance on a map and the corresponding distance on the ground.

Types of Scale

  • Statement scale
  • Representative fraction / ratio scale
  • Graphic or bar scale

8.3.1 Statement Scale

A statement scale expresses the relationship using words.

Example:

1 cm represents 5 km

This means that 1 centimetre on the map represents 5 kilometres on the ground.

8.3.2 Representative Fraction

A representative fraction expresses scale as a ratio without using units.

RF = Map Distance / Ground Distance
Example:

1 : 100,000

This means 1 unit on the map represents 100,000 of the same units on the ground.

1 cm on map = 100,000 cm on ground

8.3.3 Graphic / Bar Scale

A graphic scale represents distance using a line divided into sections with distance values.

The main advantage of a graphic scale is that it can remain useful when the map is enlarged or reduced proportionally.

8.4 Scale Conversion

Scale conversion means changing one form of scale into another form.

Important Unit Conversions

Unit Equivalent
1 km 1000 m
1 m 100 cm
1 km 100,000 cm
1 m 1000 mm
Ground Distance = Map Distance × Scale Denominator
Example:

Scale = 1 : 100,000

Map distance = 5 cm

Ground distance:

5 × 100,000 = 500,000 cm

500,000 cm = 5 km

8.5 Measuring Distance on Maps

Distance can be measured on a map using a ruler, string, divider or other appropriate tools.

Straight Distance

For a straight road or direct distance, measure the line with a ruler and convert it using the map scale.

Curved Distance

For curved roads, rivers or boundaries, a piece of string can be used to follow the curve. The length of the string is then measured with a ruler.

Example:

Map scale = 1 : 50,000

Map distance = 4 cm

Ground distance = 4 × 50,000 cm

= 200,000 cm

= 2 km

8.6 Measuring Area on Maps

Area is the amount of surface covered by a geographic feature.

Regular Shapes

For simple shapes, mathematical formulas can be used.

Rectangle Area = Length × Width
Square Area = Side × Side

Irregular Shapes

Irregular areas can be estimated by dividing them into smaller regular shapes or using a grid.

Example:

A map can be covered with a grid of equal squares. Count the complete squares and estimate the partial squares to approximate the total area.

8.7 Direction on Maps

Direction helps us describe the position of one place relative to another.

Four Cardinal Directions

  • North (N)
  • South (S)
  • East (E)
  • West (W)

Four Intermediate Directions

  • Northeast (NE)
  • Southeast (SE)
  • Southwest (SW)
  • Northwest (NW)
🧠 Remember:

N → E → S → W

Moving clockwise around a compass.

8.9 Grid References

Grid reference is a method of locating a specific position on a map using grid lines.

Grid references are especially useful for accurately identifying locations on maps.

Basic Rule

🧠 Remember:

Read Eastings first, then Northings.

"Along the corridor, then up the stairs."

8.9.1 Eastings

Eastings are vertical grid lines. Their numbers increase toward the east.

When giving a grid reference, the easting is normally given before the northing.

8.9.2 Northings

Northings are horizontal grid lines. Their numbers increase toward the north.

Example:

A four-figure grid reference might be written as: 2345

23 → Easting

45 → Northing

8.10 Map Sketching

Sketch map is a simplified drawing showing important features and their relative locations.

Characteristics

  • Simple
  • Clear
  • Shows important features
  • Uses appropriate symbols
  • Shows direction
  • May include a scale
  • Includes a title
  • Includes a key when necessary

Steps

  1. Choose the area to represent.
  2. Identify the important features.
  3. Choose a suitable orientation.
  4. Draw the main outline.
  5. Add important features.
  6. Add symbols.
  7. Add title, scale and direction.
  8. Add a key if needed.

8.11 Map Symbols

Maps use symbols because it is difficult to draw every real-world feature exactly as it appears.

Examples

  • Road symbol
  • Railway symbol
  • River symbol
  • Settlement symbol
  • Airport symbol
  • Boundary symbol
  • School symbol
  • Hospital symbol
Important:

Always check the map legend because symbols can vary between maps.

8.12 Interpreting Physical Features on Maps

Physical features are natural features of the Earth's surface.

Examples

  • Mountains
  • Hills
  • Valleys
  • Rivers
  • Lakes
  • Plains
  • Plateaus

Questions to Ask

  • Where is the feature located?
  • What direction does it extend?
  • How large is it?
  • What is its relationship with nearby features?
  • How does the feature influence human activities?

8.13 Interpreting Human Features

Human features are features created or significantly modified by people.

Examples

  • Roads
  • Railways
  • Buildings
  • Cities
  • Farms
  • Bridges
  • Dams
  • Industrial areas

Questions to Ask

  • Where are settlements concentrated?
  • Where are major roads located?
  • How are settlements connected?
  • What physical factors influence the distribution?

8.14 Interpreting Tables

Table presents information in rows and columns so that data can be organized and compared easily.

How to Read a Table

  1. Read the title.
  2. Identify the variables.
  3. Check the units.
  4. Compare values.
  5. Identify the highest and lowest values.
  6. Look for patterns.
  7. Draw a conclusion.
Example:
City Population
A 100,000
B 250,000
C 150,000

City B has the largest population in this example.

8.15 Interpreting Graphs

Graphs represent data visually and help us identify patterns and relationships.

Common Types

  • Line graph
  • Bar graph
  • Pie chart
  • Histogram
  • Population pyramid

8.15.1 Line Graph

A line graph is commonly used to show change or trends over time.

Examples

  • Population growth
  • Rainfall over months
  • Temperature changes
  • River discharge over time
🧠 Exam Tip:

When interpreting a line graph, look for the highest point, lowest point, increasing trend, decreasing trend and periods of stability.

8.15.2 Bar Graph

A bar graph uses separate bars to compare different categories.

Examples

  • Population of different regions
  • Crop production
  • Number of students
  • Annual exports

8.15.3 Pie Chart

Pie chart is a circular diagram divided into sectors representing parts of a whole.
Sector angle = Percentage × 360° / 100
Example:

If agriculture represents 25% of an economy:

25 × 360 ÷ 100 = 90°

Therefore, the agriculture sector would have a 90° sector.

8.16 Interpreting Diagrams

Diagrams use drawings and visual representations to explain geographic information or processes.

Examples

  • River drainage diagrams
  • Population pyramids
  • Cross-sections
  • Climate diagrams
  • Flow diagrams

Interpretation Steps

  1. Read the title.
  2. Identify labels.
  3. Understand the symbols.
  4. Identify relationships.
  5. Compare the components.
  6. Draw a conclusion.

8.17 Collecting Geographic Information

Geographic information can be collected from different sources.

Primary Sources

Information collected directly by the researcher.

  • Observation
  • Interview
  • Questionnaire
  • Field measurement
  • Field survey

Secondary Sources

Information already collected and published by others.

  • Books
  • Maps
  • Reports
  • Government statistics
  • Websites
  • Research publications

8.18 Fieldwork

Fieldwork is the collection of geographic information through direct investigation in the field.

Fieldwork Steps

  1. Choose a topic.
  2. Define the research question.
  3. Plan the investigation.
  4. Collect information.
  5. Record observations.
  6. Analyze the data.
  7. Draw conclusions.
  8. Present the findings.
Safety:

Fieldwork should be properly planned. Students should follow safety instructions and avoid dangerous areas.

8.19 Organizing and Analyzing Geographic Data

Collected information should be organized before it can be interpreted.

Methods

  • Tables
  • Graphs
  • Maps
  • Charts
  • Diagrams
  • Statistical calculations

Questions During Analysis

  • What does the data show?
  • What is the highest value?
  • What is the lowest value?
  • What trend is visible?
  • Are there differences between places?
  • What factors may explain the pattern?

8.20 Presenting Geographic Findings

The results of geographic enquiry should be clearly communicated to others.

Methods

  • Written report
  • Oral presentation
  • Map
  • Graph
  • Poster
  • Table
  • Chart
A good geographic presentation should be:
  • Clear
  • Accurate
  • Well organized
  • Supported by evidence
  • Easy to understand

🗺️ 8.21 Map Reading Checklist

When you see a map in an exam:

  1. Read the title.
  2. Check the legend.
  3. Check the scale.
  4. Find the north direction.
  5. Check coordinates or grid.
  6. Identify physical features.
  7. Identify human features.
  8. Compare locations.
  9. Measure distance if required.
  10. Give a conclusion based on evidence.

🧠 8.23 Exam Tricks

1. Map components
Title + Scale + Direction + Key + Symbols

2. Scale
Map distance → multiply by scale denominator to get ground distance when units are consistent.

3. Grid reference
Eastings first → Northings second.

4. Directions
N, E, S, W + NE, SE, SW, NW.

5. Line graph
Best for trends and change over time.

6. Bar graph
Best for comparing categories.

7. Pie chart
Shows parts of a whole.

8. Geographical enquiry
Question → Collect → Organize → Analyze → Interpret → Conclusion → Communicate.
