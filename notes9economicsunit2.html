📖 Unit Introduction

Human wants are unlimited, but the resources available to satisfy those wants are limited. Because of this situation, every individual, household, business and government has to make choices.

This unit explains the basic economic problems created by scarcity and the different economic systems societies use to organize their economic activities.

Main idea:
Scarcity → Choice → Opportunity Cost → Basic Economic Problems

2.1 Scarcity

Meaning of Scarcity

Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem that occurs because human wants are unlimited while resources available to satisfy those wants are limited.

Scarcity = Limited resources + Unlimited wants

Scarcity exists everywhere. Individuals, families, firms and governments all face scarcity because they cannot satisfy every want at the same time.

Examples of Scarcity

  • A student has limited money but many things to buy.
  • A family has limited income but many needs.
  • A farmer has limited land.
  • A government has limited public revenue.
  • A business has limited capital.
  • Time is limited for everyone.
Example:
If you have 100 birr and want to buy a book for 80 birr and food for 50 birr, your money is not enough to buy both. This is scarcity.

Why is scarcity a basic economic problem?

Because resources are scarce, people cannot have everything they want. Therefore, they must decide which wants to satisfy first.

2.2 Choice

Meaning of Choice

Choice means selecting one alternative from several available alternatives because resources are scarce.

Every economic decision involves choosing between alternatives.

Examples

  • A student chooses between studying and entertainment.
  • A family chooses between buying clothes and saving money.
  • A farmer chooses which crop to grow.
  • A government chooses how to allocate its budget.
  • A firm chooses what goods to produce.
Remember:
Scarcity makes choice necessary.

2.3 Opportunity Cost

Meaning

Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative that must be given up when a choice is made.

Opportunity Cost = Next best alternative forgone

Important points

  • Opportunity cost exists because resources are scarce.
  • Choice creates opportunity cost.
  • It refers to the next best alternative, not every alternative.
  • It can involve money, time, resources or other benefits.

Example 1

You have 100 birr. You can buy a book or a shirt. You choose the book. The shirt is the next best alternative you gave up. Therefore, the opportunity cost of the book is the benefit you would have received from the shirt.

Example 2

You have two hours. You can study or watch a movie. If you choose to study, the enjoyment from watching the movie may be your opportunity cost.

Opportunity Cost and Money

Opportunity cost is not always equal to the amount of money spent. It is the value of the next best alternative forgone.

2.4 The Basic Economic Problems

Because resources are scarce, every economy must answer three fundamental questions.

  1. What to produce?
  2. How to produce?
  3. For whom to produce?
The three basic economic questions:

What? → How? → For whom?

2.5 What to Produce?

This question concerns which goods and services should be produced and in what quantities.

Examples

  • Should farmers produce wheat or maize?
  • Should a firm produce shoes or clothes?
  • Should the government spend more on schools or roads?
  • How much food should be produced?

Because resources are limited, producing more of one good may mean producing less of another.

If a farmer uses all available land to grow maize, the farmer cannot use the same land at the same time to grow wheat.

2.6 How to Produce?

This question concerns the method or technique used to produce goods and services.

A producer may choose between using more labour or more machines, depending on available resources, technology and costs.

Examples

  • Should a factory use machines or workers?
  • Should farming use traditional tools or modern machinery?
  • Which technology should a business use?

Labour-intensive production

A labour-intensive technique uses relatively more human labour than capital.

Capital-intensive production

A capital-intensive technique uses relatively more machines, equipment and other capital resources.

Labour-intensive Capital-intensive
Uses relatively more workers. Uses relatively more machines.
Suitable where labour is relatively abundant. Suitable where capital and technology are available.
Human effort is important. Machines and equipment are important.

2.7 For Whom to Produce?

This question concerns how produced goods and services are distributed among members of society.

It asks who will receive and consume the goods and services produced.

Examples

  • Who will receive available housing?
  • Who can afford the goods produced?
  • How should income and resources be distributed?
A country may produce enough food, but an important question is how that food will be distributed among different groups of people.

2.8 Economic Systems

An economic system is the way a society organizes the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services and makes decisions about the use of scarce resources.

The major economic systems are:

  1. Traditional economy
  2. Market economy
  3. Command economy
  4. Mixed economy

2.9 Traditional Economic System

Meaning

A traditional economic system is an economic system in which economic decisions are mainly based on customs, traditions and practices passed from one generation to another.

Characteristics

  • Customs strongly influence economic decisions.
  • Traditional occupations are common.
  • Production methods may be simple.
  • Families and communities play important roles.
  • Change may occur slowly.

Advantages

  • Strong community relationships.
  • Economic roles are often clearly understood.
  • Traditional knowledge is preserved.
  • People may cooperate closely.

Disadvantages

  • Limited technological development.
  • Low productivity may occur.
  • Economic growth may be slow.
  • Traditional practices may limit innovation.

2.10 Market Economic System

Meaning

A market economic system is an economic system in which major economic decisions are made mainly through the interaction of buyers and sellers in markets.

Main features

  • Private ownership is important.
  • Individuals and firms make many economic decisions.
  • Prices are largely determined by demand and supply.
  • Profit is an important incentive.
  • Competition is important.

Advantages

  • Encourages competition.
  • Encourages innovation.
  • Provides consumer choice.
  • Can encourage efficient resource use.
  • Profit motivates production.

Disadvantages

  • Income inequality may increase.
  • Some people may not afford essential goods.
  • Market failures can occur.
  • Unemployment may occur.
  • Businesses may create negative external effects.

2.11 Command Economic System

Meaning

A command economic system is an economic system in which major economic decisions are made mainly by the government or central planning authority.

Main features

  • Government plays a major role in resource allocation.
  • Production decisions are centrally planned.
  • Government ownership may be important.
  • Prices may be controlled by the government.
  • Private economic decisions may be limited.

Advantages

  • Government can direct resources toward national priorities.
  • Basic services may be emphasized.
  • Income distribution can be influenced by government policies.
  • Large national projects can be planned centrally.

Disadvantages

  • Consumer choice may be limited.
  • Innovation may be reduced.
  • Central planning can be difficult.
  • Shortages or surpluses may occur.
  • Government decisions may be inefficient.

2.12 Mixed Economic System

Meaning

A mixed economic system combines elements of market and command economic systems.

Both private individuals and businesses and the government participate in economic decision-making.

Main features

  • Private ownership exists.
  • Government ownership may also exist.
  • Markets play an important role.
  • Government regulates economic activities.
  • Government provides public goods and services.
  • Economic policies can influence income distribution.

Advantages

  • Combines market incentives with government intervention.
  • Consumer choice can be maintained.
  • Government can provide essential public services.
  • Government can address some market failures.
  • Competition and private initiative can be encouraged.

Disadvantages

  • Too much government intervention may reduce incentives.
  • Too little regulation may create market problems.
  • Government policies can sometimes be inefficient.
  • Balancing public and private interests can be difficult.

2.14 How Economic Systems Answer the Three Questions

Economic Question Meaning
What to produce? Which goods and services and how much should be produced?
How to produce? Which resources and production techniques should be used?
For whom to produce? How should produced goods and services be distributed?

Different economic systems answer these questions differently.
